\begin{singlespace}\small

\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Motivation.}} Anthropogenic warming is making extreme-heat events more frequent, and large road races increasingly take place in conditions their fields did not train for. Across the six Abbott World Marathon Majors, abnormally hot editions remain rare but have arrived roughly six times more often per year since 2020 than in the preceding four decades. Finish-time prediction has improved over the same period, drawing on the training data now collected passively by GPS watches, but the predictors in current use remain weather-blind, and are therefore systematically optimistic on precisely the days when a runner most needs a dependable estimate.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Literature.}} Heat slows distance runners nonlinearly, with performance roughly flat through a cool optimum near 10--15\,\textdegree C wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and degrading increasingly above it. The cost is not the same for everyone. Slower runners in the mass field lose two to three times more pace per degree than the elite field does. That gradient appears to reflect longer time on course, larger body size, and habitual climate rather than finishing position itself, and the penalty grows with race distance. Sensitivity also depends on what a runner has recently trained in. Over 5--14 days of exposure the body adapts, expanding plasma volume and lowering heart rate and core temperature at a given effort, and that adaptation decays within one to a few weeks once exposure stops. Large observational studies rarely model this individual variation directly, handling it through geographic proxies if at all, while the controlled experiments that do measure it individually run at samples of 10--30 participants and seldom connect to race performance.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Methods.}} We assembled an observational corpus of roughly a quarter million Garmin training runs, each enriched with the weather it was run in. A transformer trained on each runner's recent streams predicts pace and, through counterfactual WBGT sweeps, yields two heat features: a population heat curve and a per-runner sensitivity score. We add these to a weather-blind XGBoost pace model (heart rate, fitness, distance, and elevation) and evaluate on a runner-disjoint held-out set of 15{,}892 activities from 104 runners, using robust MAPE and signed bias with runner-clustered bootstrap intervals, plus a direct affine calibration of the heat features.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Results.}} Adding the population heat curve lowered robust held-out error modestly (MAPE from 7.34 to 7.29\%), and adding the per-runner sensitivity score lowered it further (to 7.13\%), with a shuffled control confirming that the personalized gain is runner-specific. The population curve is well calibrated, compatible with what the model needs. The personalized score provides a genuine predictive signal, mostly acting as a runner-level calibration offset rather than a temperature interaction. This effect is nearly flat across WBGT, only about 55\% survives when forced through the temperature curve, and its residual direction is reversed, plausibly because heart rate in the baseline already carries heat sensitivity.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Implications.}} A population heat adjustment can be applied to finish-time predictions now, letting runners see how forecast conditions are likely to change their expected pace; it is well calibrated and agrees with an independent coaching heuristic. Individualized heat correction is predictive but not yet a directly applicable adjustment, and remains a question for future work, pending a baseline with an explicit mediation structure and, above all, race-day performance labels. The appropriate accuracy metric is calibration on hot runs rather than average error, because the heat effect is small next to day-to-day training variation.

\end{singlespace}

\section{Introduction}

Anthropogenic climate change has at least doubled the likelihood of record-breaking hot years globally since the late 1960s \citep{king2017}. Over the same period the share of global land area affected by moderate-to-extreme heat waves tripled, and severe heat waves are projected to recur biannually in major population centers by the century's end \citep{russo2014}.

Marathons expose tens of thousands of runners to whatever conditions race day delivers, and the six World Marathon Majors (Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago, New York City, and Tokyo) show a marked increase in the frequency of unusually hot editions. As Figure~\ref{fig:marathon_starts_trend} shows, only three of the 211 races held between 1980 and 2019 exceeded a wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) of 65 °F (18 °C), a rate of about 0.08 per year. Three of the 29 races held between 2020 and 2026 already have, a rate of roughly 0.43 per year and a sixfold increase.\footnote{WBGT was estimated using Open-Meteo reanalysis with the Stull (2011) wet-bulb formula and a globe-temperature approximation following \citet{hunter1999}. Fixing the lookup at 10\,am local gives a consistent cross-decade comparison but does not perfectly match on-course exposure, since gun times have shifted over the decades.} The Chicago Marathon is the clearest individual case, with three of its hottest editions falling in the 2020s.

Those conditions confound an already difficult prediction problem. An accurate projection matters to the millions who attempt the distance each year, since it underwrites pacing, goal-setting, and training adjustment. Classic practitioner formulas, including Yasso 800s and VDOT \citep{yasso2008life, gaudette2012yasso, daniels2013running}, remain unpublished in peer-reviewed form decades after their inception. The growth of GPS watches among runners, which track their position, heart rate, and altitude consistently throughout training, offers exciting new pathways for generalizable computational models. 

What every one of these approaches shares, including the predictors now shipped with consumer running platforms \citep{garmin_race_predictor, strava_performance_predictions}, is that it is weather-blind. \citet{vernon2021} documented the cost directly: at the warm 2018 London Marathon (24\,\textdegree C maximum), mass-participation runners finished roughly 47 minutes slower than their pre-race expectations on average, about 14\% off target. A predictor that ignores race-day conditions is therefore systematically optimistic on precisely the days when a runner most needs a dependable estimate.

This paper asks whether that missing weather term can be learned from passively collected training data. We assemble an observational corpus of roughly a quarter of a million Garmin training runs, each matched to the weather in which it was run, and train a transformer on each runner's recent training streams to predict pace. Sweeping temperature counterfactually through the trained model yields two heat features: a population heat curve, describing the average pace penalty as conditions warm, and a per-runner sensitivity score. We then test whether either feature improves a weather-blind pace model on runners held out entirely from training, and we calibrate both against the correction that model actually requires. Section~\ref{sec:literature} synthesizes the evidence on heat effects and acclimation that motivates this design, Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data and the held-out experiment, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws out their implications and what remains open.

\section{How Heat Affects Endurance Running Performance}
\label{sec:literature}

Heat degrades endurance running performance through a chain that begins with a physical constraint on heat dissipation, passes through cardiovascular and central strain, and ends in a pacing decision. This section follows that chain, then turns to why the resulting effect is concentrated in endurance events, how it has been measured and modeled, and how it varies across runners.

Running is thermally expensive. Roughly 75 to 80\% of metabolic energy turnover during exercise appears as heat rather than as mechanical work. That heat must either be dissipated or stored in body tissue \citep{maughan2007}. Storage raises core temperature, and the margin available is narrow, since enzymatic function is near optimal around 39\,\textdegree C while deterioration sets in above roughly 40.5\,\textdegree C \citep{martin2007}. Competitive runners operate close to that ceiling in the heat. Among finishers of the 2019 World Athletics road races, held at WBGT between 23.5 and 30.6\,\textdegree C, 16\% completed their event with a core temperature above 40\,\textdegree C, with a peak recorded value of 40.7\,\textdegree C \citep{aylwin2023}.

The body sheds this heat mainly by evaporating sweat. Environmental conditions create the ceiling on how much evaporation is possible. Dry exchange through radiation and convection depends on the gradient between skin and air. That gradient reverses once air temperature exceeds skin temperature at roughly 36\,\textdegree C, beyond which the runner gains heat from the environment rather than losing it \citep{cheuvront2001}. Humidity constrains the evaporative route directly. Modeling world-record performances at 33\,\textdegree C and 50\% relative humidity, \citet{taylor2006} calculated an average required evaporation of about 4{,}200\,kJ against a maximum achievable evaporation of only 1{,}100\,kJ, so barely a quarter of the sweat needed for thermal balance could actually evaporate. Sweat produced beyond that ceiling drips rather than cools, and represents fluid lost for no thermal return. Radiation adds a load that air temperature does not capture at all. The difference of about 73\,W\,m$^{-2}$ that \citet{cheuvront2021} measured between early and late Boston Marathon start windows is equivalent to roughly one metabolic equivalent of extra heat production for a four-hour finisher.

The strain this imposes is cardiovascular before it is thermal, as skin blood flow directed to the surface for cooling competes with muscle for a finite cardiac output. Dehydration compounds the problem: beyond a 2\% loss of body weight, each further 1\% is associated with roughly a 0.4\,\textdegree C rise in rectal temperature during prolonged running. Sweat rates scale with conditions as well, averaging 0.83\,L\,h$^{-1}$ in marathons below 15\,\textdegree C against 1.2\,L\,h$^{-1}$ above it \citep{cheuvront2001}.

For a competitive runner, these strains express themselves in pace. In a simulated half marathon at WBGT 25.8\,\textdegree C, running speed and oxygen uptake fell significantly once core temperature reached about 39\,\textdegree C, which occurred between 6 and 9\,km, after which core temperature plateaued for the remainder of the race \citep{rodrigues2020}. Real race data corroborate this finding. \citet{ely2008} found that the gap between a runner's closing and opening 5\,km pace widened systematically as conditions warmed while \citet{maughan2007} report that post-marathon core temperature correlates with the time for the final 10\,km rather than with the overall finishing time. Core temperature climbs toward a ceiling over the opening miles and is then held near it by the runner conceding speed. Heat, therefore, acts less as a fixed penalty than as a constraint that tightens as the race proceeds that is paid most heavily in the closing stages. 

That accumulation logic extends across events. Comparing seven World Championships from 1999 to 2011, spanning 18.5 to 30\,\textdegree C, \citet{guy2014} found the sign of the effect reverses with distance: sprinters were substantially faster in the heat ($d = 2.4$ and $2.3$ for men at 100 and 200\,m) while the marathon was the most impaired event of all, at $-3.1\%$ for men and $-2.7\%$ for women.

Sports scientists have converged on wet-bulb globe temperature as the most accurate predictor of heat-related performance stress. The standard formula is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WBGT} = 0.7\,T_{\mathrm{nwb}} + 0.2\,T_{\mathrm{bg}} + 0.1\,T_{\mathrm{db}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{nwb}}$ is the natural wet-bulb temperature, measured by a thermometer with a wet cloth wick ventilated by ambient wind and so capturing humidity and air movement together, $T_{\mathrm{bg}}$ is the black-globe temperature, measured inside a matte-black copper sphere and so capturing radiant load, and $T_{\mathrm{db}}$ is ambient air temperature \citep{cheuvront2021}. This weighting follows the physiology of heat's effect on performance described above. The most weight corresponds to the humidity-sensitive term, as evaporative capacity is the binding constraint, while dry heat alone is comparatively benign. WBGT has been found to outperform raw air temperature in cross-event comparison \citep{mantzios2022, ely2007, aylwin2023}.  

The exact functional form of the heat penalty remains contested, and Table~\ref{tab:forms} collects those that have been published. Linear models dominate the literature and adequately describe the moderate ranges observed at most major marathons \citep{ely2007, knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019, vernon2021}.\footnote{These moderate ranges are partly an artifact of major marathons being canceled once heat passes a threshold.} A linear form cannot capture the acceleration of the penalty at the hot end, however, and several designs recover it. Quadratic fits appear across single-race and multi-race designs \citep{martin1999, elhelou2012, gasparetto2020}, while the largest synthesis is explicitly piecewise: across 1{,}258 elite races, \citet{mantzios2022} reported a flat band up to 15\,\textdegree C WBGT with performance declining by $0.4 \pm 0.4\%$ per \textdegree C above it. Table~\ref{tab:forms} collects these forms.

The estimated optimal temperature for endurance running performance is also a source of scholarly contention. Optimal air temperature estimates range from about 3.8 to 9.9\,\textdegree C \citep{elhelou2012} and 5 to 10\,\textdegree C \citep{llewellyn2025} through 10 to 12\,\textdegree C \citep{maughan2010} to 12 to 15\,\textdegree C \citep{martin1999, martin2007}. On WBGT, \citet{mantzios2022} place the marathon optimum at 7.5\,\textdegree C. \citet{gasparetto2020} locate a tipping point near 8\,\textdegree C. This wide span reflects, in part, the vast individual-level differences among runners that together constitute heat tolerance.

The most studied of these individual variations is performance level. Across nearly every large observational study, slower runners lose more pace per degree than faster ones. \citet{ely2007} found that between the coolest and warmest WBGT quartiles, the 25th, 50th, 100th and 300th finishers slowed by 2.6, 3.5, 4.9 and 7.9\% respectively. At the Boston Marathon, \citet{nikolaidis2019} estimated 1 minute 53 seconds of slowing per \textdegree C for all finishers against 38 seconds for the top ten. \citet{knechtle2021b} found that running the New York City marathon in warm temperatures added 7.73 minutes for the average finisher but only 1.87 minutes for top-ten finishers. This finding has been challenged by other studies, however, which find that faster finishes have a strong pace penalty in hot weather \citep{gasparetto2020, knechtle2021a}.

Why slower runners should lose remains unsettled. The simplest explanation is exposure: a runner who is on course longer absorbs more of the day's thermal and solar load and often runs through its hottest hours. Physique supplies a second. Among marathoners with laboratory-measured fitness, percent body fat predicted how far a runner finished from their predicted time in warm conditions ($r = 0.503$) \citep{roach2012}. A third consideration corresponds to heat tolerance. Across marathon studies, faster runners generate more metabolic heat and record a hotter post-race rectal temperature \citep{cheuvront2001}. Elite runners therefore carry the larger thermal load and still lose less time to it. What separates the elite then from the mass field is not a lighter heat burden but a greater capacity to continue running at race pace while carrying it.

Other individual-level variables regarding heat sensitivity have produced mixed results. \citet{elhelou2012} found the performance gap between men and women stable across temperatures and \citet{knechtle2021b} found no significant temperature-by-sex interaction, while \citet{roach2012} and \citet{llewellyn2025} report women finishing further from their predicted times than men in warm conditions. For age, the largest heat effects appear in middle-aged rather than the oldest runners \citep{knechtle2021b, weiss2022a}, a pattern that plausibly reflects racing intensity rather than age itself.

Heat imposes the same thermodynamic constraint on every runner yet the cost it exacts varies widely. Finishing position, body composition, sex, and age each capture part of that variation---each an observable correlates of a latent heat tolerance. This unobserved quality motivates our approach of inferring heat tolerance from a runner's own history of running in varied conditions rather than using demographic proxies.

\subsection{Heat Acclimation}

The differences described above are semi-sticky attributes of a runner. Performance level and body morphology shift slowly, if at all, across a training block. They describe who a runner is rather than what that runner has recently done. Heat acclimation is different in kind. It is a shorter-term physiological response to a training stimulus, acquired within days and lost within weeks. It is the lever of heat tolerance that a runner can most rapidly and deliberately change.

Repeated exposure to heat produces a coordinated set of adaptations that make the same effort thermally cheaper. Plasma volume expands by roughly 7--18\%, resting core temperature falls, sweating begins earlier and loses less sodium, and skin blood flow responds sooner, so at a fixed workload an acclimatized runner shows a lower heart rate, a lower core temperature, and a lower perceived effort together \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, racinais2017, gibson2020}. The practical consequence is an increased thermoregulatory effect that allows a runner to hold a given pace longer \citep{marino2008, rodrigues2020}. The adaptation can be induced three ways: by exercising in the heat, by passive heating such as sauna or hot-water immersion, or simply by living and training in a hot climate. All three work over a span of about 5--14 days, with hot-climate residence combined with training producing the most complete response \citep{garrett2011, periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, heathcote2018, pryor2019, schmit2017, brown2022}.

Research generally agrees on the onset of heat acclimation and its decay. Heart-rate and core-temperature adaptations develop fastest, reaching about 75\% of their eventual gain within 4--7 days; plasma volume expands over 3--6 days; sweat-rate adaptations continue to develop through 10--14 days \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, racinais2020, chalmers2014, cubel2024}. Decay runs on a similar order once exposure stops, at roughly 2.5\% per day for the heart-rate channel, though re-acclimation is considerably faster than initial induction \citep{daanen2018, racinais2020, pryor2019}. A block of 10--14 days therefore captures most of the available adaptation, and a single session every 5--7 days maintains it \citep{daanen2018, garrett2011}. A runner's acclimation state can accordingly be approximated from training-temperature history over the trailing two to four weeks, weighting recent sessions more heavily. Across 29 studies \citet{daanen2018} fit heart-rate decay as $3.6 + 2.3$ per day without heat exposure and core-temperature decay at $2.6$ per day, both in percent of the adaptation gained. Sweat rate behaves differently, since its decay equation carries no term for elapsed days and depends instead on the length and intensity of the original acclimation block, so a longer or hotter block yields a more durable adaptation.

Most large-scale observational studies handle heat adaptation through geographic or fitness proxies such as climate of origin or tropical residence, and almost none fit an individual-level acclimation state \citep{ioannou2018, scheer2021, knechtle2021a, tan2022}. The exception is \citet{vernon2021}, who computed the difference between race-day maximum temperature and each runner's rolling 60-day prior training-environment temperature and reported a correlation of $r = 0.85$ between that differential and mean finish time, with about 2.6\% additional slowdown per 5 °C of positive differential.

Heat acclimation effects are well established in experimental settings. The largest meta-analysis, pooling 1{,}531 participants across controlled trials, reports that acclimation improves subsequent exercise performance and capacity in the heat (Hedges' $g = 0.7$) while lowering resting core temperature ($g = -0.6$) and heart rate ($g = -0.5$) and raising sweat rate ($g = 0.4$) \citep{tyler2024}. Trials in runners recover the same result. \citet{james2017} assigned 17 runners to either short-term heat acclimation or work-matched training in cool conditions, and found the acclimated group improved 5\,km time-trial performance in the heat by 6.2\% against 0.6\% for controls ($p = 0.029$), alongside a 20.8\% gain in total run distance. 

Sustained over years rather than weeks, this dynamic process hardens into something closer to a fixed trait. A runner who lives and trains in a hot climate carries an adaptation that never fully decays, because the exposure never stops. At the Marathon des Sables, a seven-day ultramarathon in the Sahara Desert, athletes from warmer countries ran 10.7\% faster overall than those from colder countries \citep{ioannou2018}. The advantage does not scale indefinitely, however: athletes from the hottest countries of origin, at 25 to 35\,\textdegree C, gained nothing over those from moderately warm countries at 15 to 25\,\textdegree C. Whether that ceiling reflects a limit on useful adaptation or the confounding of origin climate with training resources cannot be settled from the design, but it is consistent with the induction literature above, in which most of the available adaptation arrives well short of the most extreme exposure. Habitual climate, then, is a slow-moving average of exposure, and race-day performance turns on the deviation from it. Measuring that deviation requires knowing the conditions a runner has actually trained in, one session at a time.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Design}

The study estimates whether a heat response learned by a deep-learning model improves pace prediction for runners the model has never seen. A weather-blind pace model is fitted first, and heat information is then supplied to it in two stages: a population heat curve that depends only on the conditions of a run, and a per-runner sensitivity score that depends on the individual. Each stage is evaluated on the same runner-disjoint holdout against negative and constrained controls and is separately calibrated against the correction the weather-blind model actually requires. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the design.

\subsection{Data}

The corpus consists of outdoor running activities recorded on Garmin devices by athletes who consented to the research use of their data. All records were anonymized before analysis, and no age, sex or body-composition information was available or used. Activities were retained if they carried a GPS trace, since indoor and treadmill runs cannot be matched to ambient conditions, and if the runner had a sufficient preceding history for the model's context window to be populated. The resulting frame comprises approximately 259{,}000 activities from 1{,}628 runners, spanning the United States, Great Britain, India, France and Australia among others, so that the upper end of the temperature range is populated by runs actually performed in heat rather than by extrapolation.

The target variable, $y_{a,i}$, is the mean pace of activity $i$ for runner $a$, in minutes per kilometre. Two properties of the corpus constrain what can be estimated from it. First, the records carry no race labels: these are training runs performed at training effort, so every quantity reported below describes held-out prediction rather than a causal physiological estimate. Second, the runs are unevenly distributed across the temperature range, and the held-out cohort spans a time-weighted WBGT of approximately $-18$ to $25\,^{\circ}$C, so estimates in the hottest bins rest on fewer observations than those in the mild ones.

Runners were partitioned by identity rather than by activity, so that no runner contributes to both model fitting and evaluation. The deep-learning model was fitted on 1{,}186 runners, with its population curve referenced against a further 750 runners contributing one averaged value each, so that prolific runners do not dominate the percentile scale. The held-out evaluation set comprises 15{,}892 activities from 104 runners who appear in no fitting stage.

\subsection{Weather enrichment}

Each activity was matched to the conditions under which it was run by querying Open-Meteo at the time and location of the run. Conditions are summarized by the wet-bulb globe temperature, computed in its radiation-aware outdoor form from measured shortwave radiation and wind speed with the globe temperature solved numerically using the \texttt{thermofeel} implementation. A closed-form shade approximation is retained only for the case in which a value must be reconstructed from the quantities a user could supply directly, namely air temperature and relative humidity.

Rather than the value at the start line, each run is characterized by $\bar{W}_{a,i}$, the time-weighted mean WBGT over its duration. This follows from the accumulation argument in Section~\ref{sec:literature}: because thermal strain builds over the effort, a run beginning in cool morning air and finishing in midday heat is described by neither endpoint alone.

\subsection{Deep-learning heat model}

The heat features come from a transformer trained on each runner's recent training streams, a factored-attention network in the style of an existing fitness-prediction model (checkpoint \texttt{singleC\_q100\_s1\_500k}). It reads each activity as a stack of per-second channels (pace, heart rate, distance, cadence, altitude, and WBGT), tokenizes and embeds each channel, sums them with a $1/\sqrt{C}$ scale, and passes them through attention that mixes information within a run and across the runner's recent runs. It predicts pace directly (Figure~\ref{fig:dlarch}). Two heat artifacts are read off the trained model by counterfactual WBGT sweeps that hold everything else fixed.

The \emph{population heat curve} is the median predicted pace penalty as WBGT is swept, anchored at $10\,^{\circ}$C and estimated from the DL-training runners only (1{,}186 runners, with a percentile reference of 750 runners, one averaged value each so prolific runners do not dominate). Its predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C is about 5.0\%.

The \emph{per-runner sensitivity score} is each runner's percentile rank of DL-predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C relative to $10\,^{\circ}$C, $\mathrm{slowdown}_{28} = 100\,(\mathrm{DL}_{28^\circ}/\mathrm{DL}_{10^\circ} - 1)$. The primary, prospective version uses a strict prior of exactly 15 preceding windows and excludes the target activity, so it is fully prospective.

\subsection{Baseline model and feature progression}

The baseline is a weather-blind XGBoost pace model with no temperature input: current-run average heart rate, trailing 30-day fitted pace, current-run distance, and elevation (gain and grade). The experiment adds the deep-learning heat information in two steps. The population step adds the DL population curve's value at the run's WBGT as one feature, which the tree model is free to use or ignore. The personalized step additionally adds the runner's sensitivity score. Two controls sharpen the reading. A \emph{shuffled control} reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, so any genuine personalization gain should vanish. A \emph{constrained variant} supplies only the percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, which forces personalization to act through the temperature curve rather than as a free runner-level number. A supplementary branch (Section~\ref{sec:results}) replaces the heart-rate-and-trailing-pace baseline with the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, to test whether the findings survive a deliberately different, pre-run baseline.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

All comparisons use the same 15{,}892-activity holdout, identical hyperparameters, and three random seeds (100, 200, 300), so that differences between models reflect the features supplied rather than the fitting procedure.

Two error measures are reported. Let $r_{a,i} = (y_{a,i} - \hat{y}_{a,i})/y_{a,i}$ denote the relative error of a prediction, signed so that a positive value means the run was predicted faster than it was actually run. Accuracy is summarized by a robust mean absolute percentage error,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum_{a,i} \min\!\left(|r_{a,i}|,\; q_{99}\right),
\end{equation}
in which $q_{99}$ is the 99th percentile of the absolute relative error, and calibration by the signed bias, the mean of $100\,r_{a,i}$ with the same trimming applied at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Trimming is applied identically to every model. Both measures are needed because they answer different questions: MAPE describes the typical size of an error, whereas bias describes whether a model is systematically optimistic, which is the failure mode that matters on a hot race day. Because activities from the same runner are not independent, all confidence intervals come from a bootstrap resampling runners rather than activities.

Prediction error alone cannot establish whether a heat feature points in the physiologically expected direction, since a flexible tree model can exploit a feature of either sign. We therefore fit a direct affine calibration of the two heat artifacts against the correction the weather-blind prediction requires,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = \hat{p}_0\left(1 + \beta + \alpha\,c_{\mathrm{pop}} + \gamma\,d_{\mathrm{pers}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_0$ is the weather-blind prediction, $c_{\mathrm{pop}}$ the population curve evaluated at the run's WBGT, and $d_{\mathrm{pers}}$ the personalized deviation. Two coefficients carry the interpretation: $\alpha$ is the scale of the population curve, with $\alpha \approx 1$ indicating that the curve is already of the right magnitude, and $\gamma$ is the direction of the personalized deviation, with $\gamma > 0$ indicating that a runner assigned a larger predicted slowdown does in fact require a slower prediction.

\subsection{Predictive performance}

Adding the deep-learning heat information to the weather-blind XGBoost improves held-out accuracy, and a negative control confirms that the personalized part is real (Figure~\ref{fig:mape_ladder}). Robust MAPE falls from 7.3381\% for the weather-blind model, to 7.2936\% when the population DL curve is added (a gain of 0.0445 points), to 7.1304\% when the per-runner sensitivity score is added on top (a further 0.1632 points, and 0.2077 points below the weather-blind model). The current-inclusive, prior, and strict-prior versions of the personalized score perform almost identically, so the gain is not an artifact of including the target activity, and the strict-prior version is the appropriate prospective estimate.

The controls behave as they should. The shuffled control, which reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, lands at 7.2996\%, essentially tied with the population model, so the personalization gain depends on matching the score to the correct runner. In runner-clustered bootstrap terms, the personalized gain beyond the population curve is 0.1632 points with a 95\% interval of [0.039, 0.308], which excludes zero, while the shuffled control's interval [$-0.051$, 0.034] straddles it. The population curve's own gain over the weather-blind model, 0.0445 points, has an interval of [$-0.009$, 0.101] that narrowly includes zero, so the population feature is a modest and only marginally significant improvement on this cohort.

\subsection{Error by environmental condition}

Stratifying the held-out error by WBGT shows where each model helps (Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp}). The weather-blind model has the classic pattern: it predicts too slow in cold and mild conditions (a signed bias near $-2\%$) and swings to too fast in the hottest bin (a bias of $+0.75\%$ in the 22 to 25\,\textdegree C band, meaning it over-predicts pace on hot runs). The population DL feature lowers MAPE across the range, including the hottest bin. The personalized model lowers MAPE in every temperature bin and places the signed bias closest to zero throughout. Its bias curve is nearly parallel to the population model's, however, which is the first sign that the personalized gain is a level shift rather than a temperature interaction. The hottest bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones, so those estimates carry more sampling uncertainty.

\subsection{Comparison with published heat-response curves}

The deep model's population heat curve can be compared to the published literature without any shared fitting (Figure~\ref{fig:heateffects}). Expressed as percent slowdown versus a $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, the published forms fall into two bands: steep slopes for amateur and mass fields (\citealp{ely2007, nikolaidis2019, knechtle2019, vernon2021}, reaching 10 to 16\% by $30\,^{\circ}$C) and shallow slopes for elite fields (\citealp{mantzios2022, martin1999, guy2014}, near 3 to 5\%). Our curve sits at the lower edge of this range, close to the elite and shallow-amateur forms and reaching about 5\% by $28\,^{\circ}$C, which is the expected place for a mixed-effort cohort whose runs are mostly training rather than racing. It also agrees with an independent coaching heuristic, which reaches about 5.6\% at $28\,^{\circ}$C, near the deep model's 72nd percentile.

\subsection{Nature of the personalized effect}

The personalized score improves prediction, but three diagnostics indicate that its contribution is largely a runner-level calibration offset rather than a heat-specific interaction, and that its residual direction is reversed.

The change the personalized score makes to the prediction is nearly flat across WBGT. Relative to the population model, it shifts the prediction by about $-0.4$ to $-0.6\%$ across temperature bins, with essentially no row-level association with WBGT ($r = -0.027$); the change is instead ordered by the runner's DL-score quintile. That is why the temperature-bin curves in Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp} are parallel.

Forcing the personalization to act through the temperature curve keeps only part of the gain. When personalization is supplied as a percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, so that its input is zero at and below the $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, the model reaches 7.2043\% robust MAPE, a gain of 0.0893 points beyond the population curve (95\% interval [0.015, 0.167], which excludes zero) with the shuffled version showing no lift. That is a real, temperature-shaped signal, but it is about 55\% of the 0.1632-point gain of the free runner rank. The remaining advantage of the free rank is consistent with general runner-level calibration information that is not specifically temperature-dependent.

A direct calibration of the DL curves against what the weather-blind model actually needs settles the direction (Figure~\ref{fig:gamma}). The population curve's scale coefficient is $\alpha = 1.078$, with a 95\% interval of [0.823, 1.344] that includes one, so the population curve is already close to the right size. The personalized deviation's coefficient is $\gamma = -1.654$, with a 95\% interval of [$-2.545$, $-0.865$] that is decisively negative. It is nearly identical across the three seeds and stays negative when the calibration is restricted to progressively hotter subsets. Conditional on the population curve, then, runners assigned a larger saved DL slowdown require a correction in the \emph{opposite} direction. The saved personalized curves are therefore not calibrated as a direct, expected-direction multiplicative layer on top of this model, which also explains why a flexible tree model can still use them, since it is free to learn the inverse mapping.

This reversal is a statement about the residual after conditioning on the baseline, not proof that the deep model's physiological ordering is backwards. The weather-blind baseline already includes average heart rate and 30-day fitted pace, and both may already encode the consequences of heat sensitivity. A genuinely heat-sensitive runner may already receive a slower prediction through an elevated heart rate, which leaves an oppositely signed residual for the personalized feature to absorb. The defensible statement is that the population DL curve is well scaled, while the personalized DL deviation is reproducible predictive information whose residual-layer association is reversed, so it should not be applied directly as an expected-direction heat multiplier without reconsidering the baseline features and the mediation structure.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the choice of baseline}

Because the reversal described above is conditional on a heart-rate-aware baseline, we re-ran the whole progression on a deliberately different baseline that removes current effort: the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, with current-run heart rate and trailing pace excluded. This branch is supplementary and cannot be read as an apples-to-apples fitness comparison, since it changes the fitness representation and drops current effort at the same time, which raises its absolute MAPE. Within the branch, the population temperature information remains useful (though its gain interval includes zero), raw WBGT produces a clearer gain, and the strict-prior personalization no longer improves beyond the population curve. The personalized $\gamma$ reversal disappears under this baseline, but the estimate is imprecise and unstable across runner folds. The reading is that the personalized result depends on the conditional baseline, and that this branch does not independently validate the personalized magnitude.

\subsection{Acclimation}

As complementary evidence, the deep model's per-runner sensitivity tracks recent heat exposure in the acclimation-consistent direction: runners with more recent hot training receive a lower sensitivity rank, and an independently curated set of matched hot runs improves after a block of heat exposure on average. These are candidate acclimation episodes, not verified physiological labels, and the held-out cohort is not large or repeatable enough near $28\,^{\circ}$C to validate individual adaptation magnitudes. The signal is consistent with acclimation being a real population-level effect while remaining too noisy to resolve as a per-person tracker.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section draws out the implications of the results for heat-adjusted pace prediction, then their limitations.

\subsection{Implications}

A weather-adjusted pace prediction lets a runner see how a given day's heat is likely to change the finish time they would otherwise expect from their fitness, and lets them explore how a warmer or cooler forecast would move that expectation. The results show that the population component of this correction is ready for such use. The deep model's population heat curve is well scaled, since its calibration coefficient is compatible with one, it modestly improves held-out prediction, and it agrees with an independent coaching heuristic and with the published literature. Individualized heat correction is a harder problem. The per-runner sensitivity score carries reproducible predictive information, but on a heart-rate-aware baseline its residual direction is reversed, so it cannot yet be applied as a direct, expected-direction adjustment. The honest reading is that a population heat adjustment can be applied to finish-time predictions now, while a validated per-runner heat adjustment awaits further work.

Several additions would sharpen the picture. A labeled race set is the most valuable, because the corpus is training runs and the experiment can only measure held-out prediction, not race-day calibration directly. A baseline whose mediation structure is explicit enough to interpret the personalized direction would resolve whether the reversed coefficient reflects heart rate already carrying heat sensitivity or a genuine problem with the score. Denser sampling of genuinely hot runs would tighten the hot-end estimates, which are thin by design because hot races are canceled or postponed. A few variables the literature flags are absent from raw GPX files but would plausibly improve prediction: body mass, since heat production scales with body mass and smaller runners are advantaged in heat \citep{marino2008, maughan2010}; biological sex, an established performance differential under heat \citep{llewellyn2025, vihma2010, kelly2023, weiss2022b}; and resting heart rate, one of the most validated acclimation markers \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, pryor2019}. Lab-only variables such as plasma volume or sweat sodium concentration cannot be obtained from consumer devices and are outside the scope of this approach. Throughout, the appropriate accuracy metric is calibration and signed bias on hot runs rather than average error over the full distribution, because most days are temperate and the heat effect is small next to run-to-run variation.

\subsection{Limitations}

The headline caveat is that this is runner-disjoint held-out prediction, not a causal estimate of heat's physiological effect. The corpus is training runs, so every number describes how well one model predicts held-out pace, and the improvements are calibration and accuracy gains rather than measured heat responses. The held-out cohort also spans WBGT only to about $25\,^{\circ}$C, and the hottest bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones, so the hot-end estimates carry the most sampling uncertainty. The reported robust MAPE and bias are clipped at the 99th (and 1st) percentiles precisely because a few large-error rows would otherwise dominate.

The personalized result is real but narrow. A runner-matched deep-learning score improves held-out prediction, and a shuffled control removes the gain, but in this specification the incremental effect is mostly temperature-independent, only about 55\% of it survives when forced through the temperature curve, and its residual-layer coefficient is reversed. Whether that reversal reflects mediation through the heart-rate and pace features in the baseline, rather than a backwards physiological ordering, cannot be settled here, because the sensitivity branch that removes current effort loses the reversal but is too imprecise to be decisive. The personalized DL score should therefore be treated as predictive runner-level information, not as a validated, directly applicable heat multiplier.

The acclimation evidence is complementary and suggestive rather than confirmatory. Recent exposure predicts lower DL sensitivity and curated hot runs improve after exposure, but the data cannot validate individual adaptation magnitudes near race-relevant heat. This document will be revised as a labeled race set becomes available and as the mediation structure of the baseline is made explicit enough to interpret the personalized calibration direction.
