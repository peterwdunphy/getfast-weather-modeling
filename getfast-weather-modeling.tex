\begingroup\small

\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Motivation.}} Anthropogenic warming is making extreme-heat events more frequent, and large road races increasingly take place in conditions their fields did not train for. Across the six Abbott World Marathon Majors, abnormally hot editions remain rare but have arrived roughly six times more often per year since 2020 than in the preceding four decades. New models have allowed runners to better predict their finishing times. However, no existing finish-time predictor accounts for abnormal heat and/or adverse weather conditions. 

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Literature.}} Heat slows distance runners nonlinearly: performance is roughly flat through a cool optimum near 10--15\,\textdegree C wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and degrades increasingly above it. Slower amateur runners lose two to three times more time per degree than those in the elite field. This penalty grows with race distance. Individual-level impact of heat varies based on previous exposure to warm temperatures in training. Over 5--14 days, heat exposure builds tolerance, as plasma volume expands and heart rate and core temperature fall at a given effort, but that adaptation decays once exposure stops.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Methods.}} We assembled an observational corpus of roughly a quarter million Garmin training runs, each enriched with the weather it was run in. A transformer trained on each runner's recent streams predicts pace and, through counterfactual WBGT sweeps, yields two heat features: a population heat curve and a per-runner sensitivity score. We add these to a weather-blind XGBoost pace model (heart rate, fitness, distance, and elevation) and evaluate on a runner-disjoint held-out set of 15{,}892 activities from 104 runners, using robust MAPE and signed bias with runner-clustered bootstrap intervals, plus a direct affine calibration of the heat features.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Results.}} Adding the population heat curve lowered robust held-out error modestly (MAPE from 7.34 to 7.29\%), and adding the per-runner sensitivity score lowered it further (to 7.13\%), with a shuffled control confirming that the personalized gain is runner-specific. The population curve is well calibrated, compatible with what the model needs. The personalized score provides a genuine predictive signal, mostly acting as a runner-level calibration offset rather than a temperature interaction. This effect is nearly flat across WBGT, only about 55\% survives when forced through the temperature curve, and its residual direction is reversed, plausibly because heart rate in the baseline already carries heat sensitivity.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Implications.}} A population heat adjustment can be applied to finish-time predictions now, letting runners see how forecast conditions are likely to change their expected pace; it is well calibrated and agrees with an independent coaching heuristic. Individualized heat correction is predictive but not yet a directly applicable adjustment, and remains a question for future work, pending a baseline with an explicit mediation structure and, above all, race-day performance labels. The appropriate accuracy metric is calibration on hot runs rather than average error, because the heat effect is small next to day-to-day training variation.

\section{Motivation}

Extreme heat events are becoming more frequent worldwide. \citet{king2017} showed that anthropogenic climate change has at least doubled the likelihood of record-breaking hot years globally since the late 1960s. \citet{russo2014} found that the share of global land area affected by moderate-to-extreme heat waves tripled between 1980--2001 and 2002--2012, and projected that severe heat waves will recur every two years in many populated regions by century's end.

Marathons expose tens of thousands of runners to whatever conditions race day delivers. Analyzing 240 marathon races held by the six World Marathon Majors (Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago, New York City, and Tokyo) from 1980 through 2026, there has been a marked increase in the frequency of unusually hot editions in the most recent decade, which we document below.

For each race we computed the wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) --- a single number combining ambient air temperature, the cooling effect of humidity (or lack of it), and solar load --- at 10\,am local time at the start line.\footnote{WBGT was estimated using Open-Meteo reanalysis with the Stull (2011) wet-bulb formula and a globe-temperature approximation following \citet{hunter1999}. Fixing the lookup at 10\,am local gives a consistent cross-decade comparison but does not perfectly match on-course exposure, since gun times have shifted over the decades.}

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_marathon_starts_trend.pdf}
  \caption{Estimated 10\,am-local WBGT at the start line of each of the six World Marathon Majors, 1980--2026. Marker shape encodes marathon; marker fill color encodes decade.}
  \label{fig:marathon_starts_trend}
\end{figure}

The headline is that average WBGT across the six majors is roughly flat: decadal means sit between 48 and 51°F (9--11°C) from the 1980s through the 2020s, and the linear trend is small. A na\"ive ``marathons are getting hotter on average'' framing is not supported. The action is in the rare hot races. Between 1980 and 2019, only three of 211 races exceeded 65 °F (18 °C) WBGT --- a rate of about 0.08 per year. Between 2020 and 2026, three of 29 races already have, a rate of about 0.43 per year, or roughly a sixfold increase. The full set of severe-heat races in the corpus: NYC 1984 (66°F/19°C), Chicago 2007 (74°F/23°C), Boston 2012 (68°F/20°C), Chicago 2021 (69°F/21°C), NYC 2022 (68°F/20°C), and Berlin 2025 (68°F/20°C). Chicago is the clearest individual signal, with three of its hottest editions falling in the 2020s. Two honest caveats temper the precision without overturning the direction. First, the recent count rests on only three events, so the implied rate carries wide uncertainty --- this is a directional signal, not a tightly estimated one. Second, several 2020--2021 majors were cancelled or shifted to atypical dates by the pandemic, which thins and distorts that window. A third, measurement caveat: these WBGT values are sampled at a fixed 10\,am local time, but gun times have drifted across the decades (modern majors often start earlier, partly to avoid heat), so the fixed-hour comparison is approximate and small level differences across eras should not be over-read. With those caveats, the clustering of hot editions in the 2020s remains consistent with the broader climate-attribution evidence cited above, and the operational point is robust to the exact rate: hot marathons are rare but appear to be arriving more often, and a predictor that handles them well is increasingly worth having.

The 2007 Chicago Marathon was halted mid-race after temperatures climbed rapidly through the morning; one runner died and the event prompted widespread changes in how organizers handle heat \citep{davey2007}. Above roughly 85 °F (29 °C) WBGT, race directors typically cancel or curtail events outright \citep{kigen2023, mantzios2022}, so observations of marathons run in genuinely dangerous heat are rare by design --- the data are censored at the hot end.

This matters for prediction because when unusually warm conditions hit, a runner's recent personal-record and training paces are no longer a reliable guide to finish time. \citet{vernon2021} documented this directly: at the unusually warm 2018 London Marathon (24 °C maximum), mass-participation runners finished roughly 47 minutes slower than their pre-race expectations on average, or about 14\% off target. Other large-population estimates of the heat penalty for amateur marathoners fall in a similar range \citep{ely2007, knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019}. For a 3:30 marathoner, that is the difference between a personal-record day and a disappointing one. Two runners with identical recent paces will not respond identically to a hot race: the runner who has been training in warm conditions tolerates heat better than the one who has not, by a margin documented in meta-analyses of heat acclimation \citep{tyler2016, pryor2019, periard2015, daanen2018}.

This paper analyzes 68 academic papers grouped into two themes: 34 papers on the direct effects of weather (especially heat) on running performance, and 34 papers on heat acclimation --- how runners adapt to repeated heat exposure and which physiological variables track that adaptation. Papers were chosen to focus primarily on the marathon distance, with secondary coverage of half-marathon, 10K, and 5K races. Section~\ref{sec:literature} synthesizes that corpus and derives the priors for the weather-adjusted pace model developed here. Section~\ref{sec:methods} then describes the data and the held-out experiment, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Section~\ref{sec:implications} draws out their implications and what remains open.

\section{Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The next three subsections synthesize the corpus: how weather (chiefly heat) degrades running performance, how runners adapt to heat and how that adaptation decays, and the explicit predictive formulas the literature offers. Together they form the evidence base for the strategic and technical decisions that follow.

\subsection{Weather effects}

\subsubsection{Temperature}

The magnitude of marathon slowdown with rising ambient temperature varies substantially across studies, and the reported coefficient depends heavily on which runners are sampled. For elite men, \citet{ely2007} found that mean finishing times slowed by about 2.8 percentage points off course record going from 5--10 °C to 20--25 °C WBGT, and \citet{guy2014} reported a 3.1\% mean slowdown for top-10 IAAF World Championship men when air temperature exceeded 25 °C. \citet{nikolaidis2019} estimated about 38 seconds slower per °C air temperature for annual top-10 finishers at Boston, and \citet{knechtle2019} reported 20 seconds per °C for annual Boston winners. Among mass-field runners the slope is consistently steeper: \citet{ely2007} reported about 3.2\% slowdown per 5 °C WBGT increase for 300th-place finishers; \citet{nikolaidis2019} found about 1:53 (min:sec) per °C for all Boston finishers; \citet{knechtle2021b} reported about eight minutes additional finishing time in the hottest NYC Marathon temperature tertile (17--24 °C) versus the coldest (5--12 °C). \citet{vernon2021} found an approximate 2.8\% slowdown per 5 °C above 12 °C for London Marathon mass participants, and \citet{tan2022} reported per-percent elasticities of roughly 7.6\% on finish time per 1\% rise in dry-bulb temperature in tropical Singapore.

The functional form is debated. Linear fits work reasonably well across the moderate temperature ranges observed at most majors and dominate the regression literature \citep{ely2007, knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019, vernon2021}. Quadratic fits with a U-shaped optimum appear in \citet{martin1999} (optimum near 54 °F / 12 °C for elite men at NYC), \citet{elhelou2012} (optima ranging from 3.8 °C to 9.9 °C across performance levels), \citet{gasparetto2020} (cluster-specific quadratics with tipping point near 8 °C WBGT), and \citet{llewellyn2025} (J-curve with optimum around 5--10 °C for median runners). \citet{mantzios2022} synthesized 1,258 elite races and reported a piecewise structure with an optimum band of 7.5--15 °C WBGT and a slope of roughly 0.3--0.4\% performance decline per °C outside that band, asymmetric across events (steeper on the hot side for racewalking, gentler for the marathon).

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_functional_forms_compared.pdf}
  \caption{How different authors have fit the WBGT-to-slowdown relationship for the marathon. Linear, quadratic, and J-shaped forms have all been published; above roughly 18 \textdegree C / 64 \textdegree F WBGT the studies diverge, and observations in genuinely dangerous heat are rare because races are typically cancelled or postponed above about 29 \textdegree C / 85 \textdegree F WBGT.}
  \label{fig:functional_forms_compared}
\end{figure}

On cold-side asymmetry, this review focuses on heat penalties. A few papers do report J-curves with modest slowdown below the optimum: \citet{martin1999} found Boston/NYC times rising at the cold end; \citet{mantzios2022} reported a cold-side slope of about 0.3\% per °C; \citet{llewellyn2025} observed the same for median runners. The present analysis does not model cold-side penalties, which are left to future work.

\subsubsection{Humidity, dew point, and composite heat indices}

The literature converges on WBGT as the most useful single composite heat metric, outperforming raw air temperature in cross-event comparisons \citep{mantzios2022, ely2007, aylwin2023}. The standard formula is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WBGT} = 0.7\,T_{\mathrm{nwb}} + 0.2\,T_{\mathrm{bg}} + 0.1\,T_{\mathrm{db}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{nwb}}$ is the natural wet-bulb temperature (a thermometer with a wet cloth wick, ventilated by ambient wind, capturing combined humidity and wind), $T_{\mathrm{bg}}$ is the black-globe temperature (a thermometer inside a matte-black copper sphere, capturing solar load), and $T_{\mathrm{db}}$ is the ambient air temperature \citep{cheuvront2021}.

Operationally, the convenient news is that Open-Meteo, the weather API used here for both forecasts and historical reanalysis, exposes two of these three terms as directly-queryable hourly variables: \texttt{wet\_bulb\_temperature\_2m} supplies the natural wet-bulb temperature $T_{\mathrm{nwb}}$, and \texttt{temperature\_2m} supplies the ambient air (dry-bulb) temperature $T_{\mathrm{db}}$. Querying $T_{\mathrm{nwb}}$ directly removes the need to estimate it from Stull's (2011) formula or any other empirical approximation; the API does the work. Only the black-globe term, $T_{\mathrm{bg}}$, must still be approximated, because no operational API exposes a measured globe temperature. A simple linear approximation, due to \citet{hunter1999} and derived for standard meteorological inputs, is:
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{bg}} \approx T_{\mathrm{db}} + 0.0083\,S - 0.005\,U,
\end{equation}
where $S$ is shortwave solar radiation in W/m\textsuperscript{2} (\texttt{shortwave\_radiation\_instant} from Open-Meteo) and $U$ is wind speed in m/s (\texttt{wind\_speed\_10m}). Combined with the directly-queried wet-bulb and ambient air temperature, this gives a complete WBGT estimator from four standard Open-Meteo hourly variables: \texttt{temperature\_2m}, \texttt{wet\_bulb\_temperature\_2m}, \texttt{shortwave\_radiation\_instant}, and \texttt{wind\_speed\_10m}. If a future Open-Meteo release exposes a built-in WBGT, it should be preferred. One caveat carries through to the rest of this document: the globe term is the least certain part of the estimate. The \citet{hunter1999} approximation was fit to general meteorological data rather than running-specific exposure, and linear globe-temperature models of this kind degrade at low solar elevation (early morning, heavy overcast, night), so $T_{\mathrm{bg}}$ is the dominant source of error in the WBGT estimate and should be checked against any available measured-globe reference. With that caveat, WBGT is practical for both forecast and historical training-run lookups in the current API surface.

For fallbacks: if shortwave radiation is unavailable for a historical timestamp, substitute total cloud cover (\texttt{cloud\_cover}) as a coarse proxy and bias-correct empirically. If both solar and wet-bulb are unavailable, fall back to the heat index (a function of air temperature and relative humidity only) or to Open-Meteo's \texttt{apparent\_temperature} variable, which integrates wind chill, humidity, and radiation in a similar spirit to WBGT but is calibrated differently.

On preferring dew point over relative humidity in regressions: \citet{vihma2010} showed that relative humidity is anti-correlated with air temperature within a race day, producing spurious negative humidity coefficients in OLS (faster times at higher humidity). \citet{knechtle2021b}, \citet{weiss2022b}, and \citet{weiss2024} reproduce this artifact at NYC and Berlin. Dew point or specific humidity avoid the confound and should be the default if humidity enters the model directly \citep{vihma2010, weiss2024}.

\subsubsection{Distance dependence}

Heat effects scale with race distance. \citet{guy2014} reported that elite marathon times slowed about 3\% in hot (>25 °C) versus temperate IAAF Championship conditions, while 5,000 m and 10,000 m times slowed only about 2\%. \citet{mantzios2022} fit per-°C-WBGT slopes that scale with event duration: about 0.2\% per °C above optimum for the marathon, 0.3\% for the 5,000 m, 0.04\% for the 10,000 m, and 0.4--1.1\% for the longer 20 km and 50 km racewalks. \citet{taylor2006} modeled the underlying physiology and concluded that heat-related performance benefits only become physiologically meaningful for events beyond about 1500 m. A rough rule of thumb from these data is that the half-marathon penalty is roughly half to three-quarters of the marathon penalty at the same WBGT; the 10K is roughly one-third. The literature directly comparing distances on the same days under matched fitness is thin --- mostly \citet{guy2014} and \citet{mantzios2022} --- so this rule of thumb should be treated as a prior, not a fact.

\subsubsection{Elite vs.\ amateur runners}

Across nearly every observational study, slower runners experience a larger percent slowdown in heat than faster runners. \citet{ely2007} reported that 25th-place finishers slowed about 1.1\% per 5 °C WBGT increase while 300th-place finishers slowed about 3.2\% per 5 °C. \citet{vihma2010} found that going from 10 °C to 25 °C added 5 minutes to an elite male marathon time but 23 minutes to a 4-hour finisher's time. \citet{nikolaidis2019} estimated 1:53 per °C for all Boston finishers versus 37--38 seconds per °C for the top 100 and top 10. \citet{knechtle2019} found similar magnitudes: 1:47 per °C for all Boston finishers versus 20 seconds per °C for annual winners. \citet{knechtle2021b} reported eight minutes of heat penalty for the NYC mass field versus under two minutes for the top 10. \citet{tan2022} reported larger Singapore Marathon temperature elasticities for slow runners than fast (regression coefficient 10.5 versus 6.9). A rough rule of thumb: amateur and back-of-pack runners are roughly 2--3 times more heat-sensitive in absolute percent-slowdown terms than elite runners.

The mechanism is partly that slower runners spend longer exposed to deteriorating midday conditions \citep{ely2007, cheuvront2021}, and partly that elite runners are smaller and lighter (lower heat production per surface area) and more likely to be heat-acclimatized through climate of origin \citep{marino2008, maughan2010, scheer2021, ioannou2018}. Two counter-examples are worth flagging: \citet{ely2008} found that within a women's championship field, the within-race pacing decay in warm conditions was actually larger for elites than for the mass field (though slower runners still ran slower overall); and \citet{gasparetto2020} found cluster-specific WBGT coefficients in which the fastest NYC cluster actually had the largest percent decrement. \citet{knechtle2021a} also reported that top-3 and top-10 finishers at Berlin run \emph{faster} on warmer days, attributed to East African dominance of the elite tier and possibly to start-time exposure windows that miss the afternoon heat. The dominant signal is still that amateurs lose more time per °C, but the elite tier is heterogeneous.

\subsubsection{Wind and other variables}

Wind and pressure receive much less attention in the corpus. \citet{nikolaidis2019} and \citet{knechtle2019} both fit wind speed effects at Boston: roughly 13--19 seconds per km/h for all finishers, dropping toward zero or slightly negative for elites. Wind direction matters on point-to-point courses: at Boston, head wind from the east cost about 11 minutes versus a tail wind from the west for all finishers, dropping to about 5 minutes for winners \citep{knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019}. On the loop courses (Berlin, Chicago, NYC), the direction effect washes out. \citet{vihma2010} found wind speed had no significant net effect on Stockholm Marathon finishing times, attributed to compensating tailwind/headwind segments. Barometric pressure shows small effects --- about 6--9 seconds per hPa at Boston and NYC \citep{knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019, weiss2024} --- but is likely confounded with temperature. Precipitation slows non-elite runners modestly \citep{knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019}.

\subsection{Heat acclimation and moderating effects}

\subsubsection{Protocols and exposure shapes}

The acclimation literature is dominated by controlled exercise-in-heat protocols, with growing attention to passive methods (sauna, hot water immersion) and natural climate residence.

Active exercise-in-heat protocols typically run 60--90 minutes per day for 5--14 days at 30--40 °C, 40--60\% relative humidity, at intensities targeting either a fixed workload (50--65\% of $\dot{V}\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{max}$) or a controlled hyperthermia endpoint of core temperature around 38.5 °C \citep{garrett2011, periard2015, gibson2020, tyler2016, tyler2024}. Short-term ($\leq$7 day), medium-term (8--14 day), and long-term ($\geq$15 day) categorizations are now standard \citep{tyler2024, kelly2023}.

Passive sauna protocols typically use 30-minute post-exercise sessions for 10--15 sessions over 2--3 weeks at sauna temperatures of roughly 176--194 °F (80--90 °C) and 10--20\% relative humidity \citep{heathcote2018, pryor2019}. Hot water immersion uses 40 °C water for 30--40 minutes post-exercise, daily for 6--14 days \citep{heathcote2018, casadio2017, chaillou2022}. Both passive modalities reliably produce plasma volume expansion of 7--18\% and reductions in resting core temperature of about 0.3 °C \citep{heathcote2018, pryor2019}.

Natural climate residence --- training in a hot location for 8--15 days --- produces the most complete adaptation when combined with active training. \citet{schmit2017} reported 12--17\% plasma volume expansion after 8 days in Guadeloupe (~30 °C, 74\% relative humidity), and \citet{charlot2017} and \citet{malgoyre2018} documented broad cardiovascular and perceptual adaptations in French soldiers over 5--15 days in the United Arab Emirates. \citet{brown2022} reviewed seasonal acclimatization across 29 studies and reported that adaptations emerge at a mean summer daytime WBGT of about 25 °C.

\subsubsection{Why heat adaptation works (physiological basis)}

The short version: with repeated exposure, the body learns to keep cooler at the same effort. Blood plasma volume expands, so the heart can pump more blood per beat and supply both the working muscles and the skin (for cooling) without the rate climbing as steeply. Sweating starts earlier in exercise, with more total volume but less sodium loss, so evaporative cooling is more effective and more sustainable. Skin blood flow becomes more responsive to a given rise in core temperature. At a fixed workload, an acclimated runner ends up with a lower heart rate, a lower core temperature, a lower perceived effort rating, and a lower sense of how hot they feel --- all together \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, racinais2017, gibson2020}.

The practical consequence is that an acclimated runner running the same pace in the same conditions has more thermoregulatory headroom and can hold that pace longer before pace must drop \citep{periard2015, marino2008, rodrigues2020}.

\subsubsection{Time course and decay}

The shape of acclimation onset and decay is now reasonably well characterized across markers. \autoref{fig:acclimation_timeline} plots parametric curves calibrated to time constants reported in the cited literature.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_acclimation_timeline.pdf}
  \caption{Time course of four major heat-acclimation markers --- onset over a 28-day acclimation block (left) and decay after exposure stops (right). Curves are parametric, calibrated to time constants reported in the cited literature.}
  \label{fig:acclimation_timeline}
\end{figure}

Heart-rate and core-temperature adaptations develop fastest, with roughly 75\% of the gain achieved in the first 4--7 days \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, racinais2020, pryor2019}. Sweat-rate and sweat-sodium adaptations emerge more slowly and continue to develop through 10--14 days and beyond \citep{tyler2016, kelly2023, mikkelsen2019, cubel2024}. Plasma volume expansion appears within 3--6 days and can reach 4--15\% by two weeks \citep{periard2015, chalmers2014, tyler2024}. Total hemoglobin mass adapts more slowly, requiring 3--5 weeks of sustained heat training \citep{cubel2024, mccleave2017}.

Decay, once exposure stops, runs at roughly 2.5\% per day for heart-rate and core-temperature adaptations \citep{daanen2018, gibson2020}, with about 35\% of the heart-rate adaptation and 30\% of the sweat-rate adaptation lost over two weeks of cool conditions \citep{daanen2018, racinais2020, pryor2019}. Plasma volume retreats faster --- often back to baseline within one week of stopping --- while hemoglobin-mass gains persist longer \citep{cubel2024, mccleave2017, chalmers2014}. Re-acclimation is asymmetric: 2--5 days of renewed heat exposure can restore most of the adaptation lost over 2--4 weeks, at roughly 8--12 times the rate of initial induction \citep{racinais2020, daanen2018, pryor2019}.

Roughly a 10--14 day acclimation block captures the bulk of available adaptation, and at least one heat session every 5--7 days maintains it \citep{daanen2018, pryor2018, pryor2019, racinais2020, garrett2011}. Operationally, a runner's acclimation state can be approximated from their training-temperature history over the trailing 14--28 days. Heart-rate-channel adaptations decay faster, so they should be weighted more heavily on recent sessions, while the blood-volume-channel adaptations decay slower and can be weighted over a longer window.

\subsubsection{How the literature models adaptation analytically}

Most large-sample observational studies treat heat adaptation implicitly through proxies rather than as an explicit model term. The dominant pattern is a geographic-or-fitness proxy: \citet{ioannou2018} used average maximum temperature in each runner's country of origin as a heat-acclimatization index for the Marathon des Sables; \citet{scheer2021} and \citet{knechtle2021a} pointed to East African origin among elite Berlin Marathon winners as an explanation for their reduced heat sensitivity; \citet{tan2022} attributed the modest heat slopes at the Singapore Marathon to the tropical-resident participant population. Almost no observational study fits an individual-level acclimation state.

The exception is \citet{vernon2021}, who computed the difference between race-day maximum temperature and each runner's rolling 60-day prior training-environment temperature and found a very large correlation ($r = 0.85$) between this differential and \emph{mean} finish time, with about 2.6\% additional slowdown per 5 °C of positive differential. Two cautions attach to that $r = 0.85$. It is computed at the race-mean (aggregate) level, describing how average finish time tracks the average temperature differential; ecological correlations of this kind are typically far larger than the individual-runner association they are taken to imply. And it comes from a single race (2018 London). It is nonetheless the closest the heat-effects corpus comes to an individual-level acclimation feature, and the differential it defines is directly aligned with the approach taken here.

Controlled experiments, in contrast, almost always work at the individual level, measuring adaptation pre- and post-intervention for each subject. But these studies have small samples (typically $n = 10$--30) and rarely connect their individual-level acclimation measurements to race-day performance prediction. The model developed here bridges these literatures: it learns an individual acclimation state for each runner from training-environment history, anchored by the literature-derived onset and decay rates as priors.

\subsection{Predictive formulas in the literature}
\label{sec:litformulas}

A handful of papers in the corpus provide explicit functional forms that can be used directly as priors.

For weather-to-performance, \citet{elhelou2012} fit quadratic functions of air temperature to top-quartile and median running speed across six major marathons and reported optimal temperatures ranging from about 39 to 50 °F (4 to 10 °C) depending on performance level. \citet{martin1999} fit a quadratic to elite men's NYC winning times: $\mathrm{WT}(\mathrm{min}) = 148.51 - 0.713\,T + 0.00657\,T^2$, with $T$ in °F, minimized at 54 °F (12 °C). \citet{vernon2021} fit two linear regressions to London Marathon mean finishing times: time (min) $= 1.5248 \cdot T_{\max} + 246.52$ for race-day maximum temperature, and time (min) $= 1.4918 \cdot \Delta T + 265.54$ for the differential between race-day maximum and prior 60-day mean maximum --- the latter is the closest in the corpus to an acclimation-aware predictor. \citet{aylwin2023} fit a linear relationship between WBGT and race-level percent of personal best across elite IAAF World Championship road races: $\%\mathrm{PB} = 2.1534 \cdot \mathrm{WBGT} + 52.513$, with $R^2 = 0.89$. \citet{gasparetto2020} fit cluster-specific quadratics in WBGT with linear coefficients ranging from $-58.75$ to $-202.9$ s/°C and quadratic coefficients from $3.79$ to $13.03$ s/°C${}^2$ across four performance clusters. These are the published forms against which we compare the deep model's learned curve in Section~\ref{sec:res_curve}.

\citet{vihma2010} fit a non-finisher-rate equation for the Stockholm Marathon:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PNF} = 0.30\,T + 0.54\,q,
\end{equation}
where $T$ is ambient air temperature in °C and $q$ is specific humidity in grams per kilogram. Vihma uses specific humidity rather than relative humidity because relative humidity is anti-correlated with air temperature and produces spurious coefficients in OLS.

For acclimation onset and decay, \citet{daanen2018} fit three regressions across 21 controlled-acclimation studies for heart-rate, core-temperature, and sweat-rate adaptation. Those regressions reference physiological channels not measured in this study, so we do \emph{not} use the coefficients as plug-in features. It takes one thing from them: the relative \emph{ordering} of decay rates across channels (heart-rate adaptation fading fastest, plasma-volume and sweat adaptations more slowly), which sets the relative half-lives of the training-history features.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The results in this paper come from a single held-out experiment: whether a deep-learning model's learned heat response improves a weather-blind pace model, first as a population temperature feature and then as runner-specific heat information. This section describes the training corpus, the deep-learning model that produces the heat features, the weather-blind baseline that consumes them, and the held-out evaluation. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} is the map.

\subsection{The training corpus}

The corpus is observational: real training runs recorded on Garmin devices, not a controlled experiment. Every outdoor run is enriched with the weather it was actually run in, queried from Open-Meteo at the run's time and location, so the table spans the United States, Great Britain, India, France, Australia, and more, and the hot end of the WBGT range is genuinely populated. The full model frame is about 259{,}000 activities from 1{,}628 runners. The held-out experiment reported here uses a runner-disjoint slice of 15{,}892 activities from 104 runners, none of whom were used to fit any model. Two facts shape the analysis. There are no race labels: these are training runs at training effort, so the results describe held-out prediction, not a causal physiological estimate. And this particular held-out cohort spans WBGT from about $-18$ to $25\,^{\circ}$C, so the hottest bins carry more sampling uncertainty than the mild ones.

We summarize each run's WBGT by its time-weighted mean over the run rather than its starting value, because a runner who starts at 6\,am in the cool and finishes in the heat should be scored by the middle of that curve. The deep model uses a radiation-aware outdoor WBGT, computed from the measured shortwave radiation and wind and solved for globe temperature with \texttt{thermofeel}; a closed-form shade approximation is used only where a value must be reconstructed from the familiar quantities a user would supply, namely air temperature and humidity.

\subsection{The deep-learning heat model}

The heat features come from a transformer trained on each runner's recent training streams, a factored-attention network in the style of an existing fitness-prediction model (checkpoint \texttt{singleC\_q100\_s1\_500k}). It reads each activity as a stack of per-second channels (pace, heart rate, distance, cadence, altitude, and WBGT), tokenizes and embeds each channel, sums them with a $1/\sqrt{C}$ scale, and passes them through attention that mixes information within a run and across the runner's recent runs. It predicts pace directly (Figure~\ref{fig:dlarch}). Two heat artifacts are read off the trained model by counterfactual WBGT sweeps that hold everything else fixed.

The \emph{population heat curve} is the median predicted pace penalty as WBGT is swept, anchored at $10\,^{\circ}$C and estimated from the DL-training runners only (1{,}186 runners, with a percentile reference of 750 runners, one averaged value each so prolific runners do not dominate). Its predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C is about 5.0\%.

The \emph{per-runner sensitivity score} is each runner's percentile rank of DL-predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C relative to $10\,^{\circ}$C, $\mathrm{slowdown}_{28} = 100\,(\mathrm{DL}_{28^\circ}/\mathrm{DL}_{10^\circ} - 1)$. The primary, prospective version uses a strict prior of exactly 15 preceding windows and excludes the target activity, so it is fully prospective.

\subsection{The weather-blind baseline and the feature progression}

The baseline is a weather-blind XGBoost pace model with no temperature input: current-run average heart rate, trailing 30-day fitted pace, current-run distance, and elevation (gain and grade). The experiment adds the deep-learning heat information in two steps. The population step adds the DL population curve's value at the run's WBGT as one feature, which the tree model is free to use or ignore. The personalized step additionally adds the runner's sensitivity score. Two controls sharpen the reading. A \emph{shuffled control} reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, so any genuine personalization gain should vanish. A \emph{constrained variant} supplies only the percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, which forces personalization to act through the temperature curve rather than as a free runner-level number. A supplementary branch (Section~\ref{sec:res_fitness}) replaces the heart-rate-and-trailing-pace baseline with the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, to test whether the findings survive a deliberately different, pre-run baseline.

\subsection{Evaluation and calibration tests}

Every comparison uses the same 15{,}892-activity holdout, the same XGBoost hyperparameters, and three seeds (100, 200, 300). Error is reported as robust MAPE (clipped at its 99th percentile) and signed bias (clipped at its 1st and 99th percentiles), applied identically to every model, with runner-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals. Beyond prediction error, we run a direct calibration test of whether the DL curves point the right way. We fit an affine correction to the weather-blind prediction, $\mathrm{pace} = \hat{p}_0\,(1 + \beta + \alpha\,c_{\mathrm{pop}} + \gamma\,d_{\mathrm{pers}})$, where $c_{\mathrm{pop}}$ is the population curve and $d_{\mathrm{pers}}$ the personalized deviation, and read two coefficients: $\alpha$, the scale of the population curve, where a value near 1 means the curve is already the right size, and $\gamma$, the direction of the personalized deviation, where a positive value means the saved DL sensitivity points the right way.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the held-out experiment in order: the main model comparison, how the error varies with temperature, how the learned heat curve compares to external references, what the personalization actually is, a sensitivity check under a different baseline, and the complementary acclimation evidence. Table~\ref{tab:mainresult} collects the headline numbers.

\subsection{The main result}
\label{sec:res_main}

Adding the deep-learning heat information to the weather-blind XGBoost improves held-out accuracy, and a negative control confirms that the personalized part is real (Figure~\ref{fig:mape_ladder}). Robust MAPE falls from 7.3381\% for the weather-blind model, to 7.2936\% when the population DL curve is added (a gain of 0.0445 points), to 7.1304\% when the per-runner sensitivity score is added on top (a further 0.1632 points, and 0.2077 points below the weather-blind model). The current-inclusive, prior, and strict-prior versions of the personalized score perform almost identically, so the gain is not an artifact of including the target activity, and the strict-prior version is the appropriate prospective estimate.

The controls behave as they should. The shuffled control, which reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, lands at 7.2996\%, essentially tied with the population model, so the personalization gain depends on matching the score to the correct runner. In runner-clustered bootstrap terms, the personalized gain beyond the population curve is 0.1632 points with a 95\% interval of [0.039, 0.308], which excludes zero, while the shuffled control's interval [$-0.051$, 0.034] straddles it. The population curve's own gain over the weather-blind model, 0.0445 points, has an interval of [$-0.009$, 0.101] that narrowly includes zero, so the population feature is a modest and only marginally significant improvement on this cohort.

\subsection{Error by temperature}
\label{sec:res_temp}

Stratifying the held-out error by WBGT shows where each model helps (Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp}). The weather-blind model has the classic pattern: it predicts too slow in cold and mild conditions (a signed bias near $-2\%$) and swings to too fast in the hottest bin (a bias of $+0.75\%$ in the 22 to 25\,\textdegree C band, meaning it over-predicts pace on hot runs). The population DL feature lowers MAPE across the range, including the hottest bin. The personalized model lowers MAPE in every temperature bin and places the signed bias closest to zero throughout. Its bias curve is nearly parallel to the population model's, however, which is the first sign that the personalized gain is a level shift rather than a temperature interaction. The hottest bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones, so those estimates carry more sampling uncertainty.

\subsection{The learned heat curve against external references}
\label{sec:res_curve}

The deep model's population heat curve can be compared to an independent coaching heuristic and to the published literature without any shared fitting (Figure~\ref{fig:dl_vs_coach}). The curve and the coaching benchmark agree in direction and in hot-end magnitude: both are anchored near zero at $10\,^{\circ}$C and reach roughly 5 to 6\% by $28\,^{\circ}$C. At $28\,^{\circ}$C the coaching value is 5.62\% and the DL median is 5.04\%, which places the heuristic near the DL 72nd percentile at that endpoint. The two shapes are not identical, since the coaching heuristic assigns more slowdown through moderate heat while the DL curve stays flatter longer and rises more sharply near $28\,^{\circ}$C. Against the published marathon fits, the DL curve sits in the mainstream: below the steep mass-field slopes of \citet{ely2007} and \citet{vernon2021} and near the shallow elite slope of \citet{mantzios2022}, which is the expected place for a mixed-effort amateur cohort.

\subsection{What the personalization is: calibration, not a heat interaction}
\label{sec:res_gamma}

The personalized score clearly improves prediction, but a set of diagnostics shows that its improvement is mostly a runner-level calibration offset rather than a heat-specific interaction, and that its residual direction is in fact reversed. Three pieces of evidence point the same way.

First, the change the personalized score makes to the prediction is nearly flat across WBGT. Relative to the population model, it shifts the prediction by about $-0.4$ to $-0.6\%$ across temperature bins, with essentially no row-level association with WBGT ($r = -0.027$); the change is instead ordered by the runner's DL-score quintile. That is why the temperature-bin curves in Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp} are parallel.

Second, forcing the personalization to act through the temperature curve keeps only part of the gain. When personalization is supplied as a percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, so that its input is zero at and below the $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, the model reaches 7.2043\% robust MAPE, a gain of 0.0893 points beyond the population curve (95\% interval [0.015, 0.167], which excludes zero) with the shuffled version showing no lift. That is a real, temperature-shaped signal, but it is about 55\% of the 0.1632-point gain of the free runner rank. The remaining advantage of the free rank is consistent with general runner-level calibration information that is not specifically temperature-dependent.

Third, a direct calibration of the DL curves against what the weather-blind model actually needs settles the direction (Figure~\ref{fig:gamma}). The population curve's scale coefficient is $\alpha = 1.078$, with a 95\% interval of [0.823, 1.344] that includes one, so the population curve is already close to the right size. The personalized deviation's coefficient is $\gamma = -1.654$, with a 95\% interval of [$-2.545$, $-0.865$] that is decisively negative. It is nearly identical across the three seeds and stays negative when the calibration is restricted to progressively hotter subsets. Conditional on the population curve, then, runners assigned a larger saved DL slowdown require a correction in the \emph{opposite} direction. The saved personalized curves are therefore not calibrated as a direct, expected-direction multiplicative layer on top of this model, which also explains why a flexible tree model can still use them, since it is free to learn the inverse mapping.

This reversal is a statement about the residual after conditioning on the baseline, not proof that the deep model's physiological ordering is backwards. The weather-blind baseline already includes average heart rate and 30-day fitted pace, and both may already encode the consequences of heat sensitivity. A genuinely heat-sensitive runner may already receive a slower prediction through an elevated heart rate, which leaves an oppositely signed residual for the personalized feature to absorb. The defensible statement is that the population DL curve is well scaled, while the personalized DL deviation is reproducible predictive information whose residual-layer association is reversed, so it should not be applied directly as an expected-direction heat multiplier without reconsidering the baseline features and the mediation structure.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the fitness baseline}
\label{sec:res_fitness}

Because the reversal in the previous section is conditional on a heart-rate-aware baseline, we re-ran the whole progression on a deliberately different baseline that removes current effort: the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, with current-run heart rate and trailing pace excluded. This branch is supplementary and cannot be read as an apples-to-apples fitness comparison, since it changes the fitness representation and drops current effort at the same time, which raises its absolute MAPE. Within the branch, the population temperature information remains useful (though its gain interval includes zero), raw WBGT produces a clearer gain, and the strict-prior personalization no longer improves beyond the population curve. The personalized $\gamma$ reversal disappears under this baseline, but the estimate is imprecise and unstable across runner folds. The reading is that the personalized result depends on the conditional baseline, and that this branch does not independently validate the personalized magnitude.

\subsection{Heat-acclimation evidence}
\label{sec:res_accl}

As complementary evidence, the deep model's per-runner sensitivity tracks recent heat exposure in the acclimation-consistent direction: runners with more recent hot training receive a lower sensitivity rank, and an independently curated set of matched hot runs improves after a block of heat exposure on average. These are candidate acclimation episodes, not verified physiological labels, and the held-out cohort is not large or repeatable enough near $28\,^{\circ}$C to validate individual adaptation magnitudes. The signal is consistent with acclimation being a real population-level effect while remaining too noisy to resolve as a per-person tracker.

\section{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

The results carry three kinds of implication: what they mean for heat-adjusted pace prediction, what would strengthen them, and what to be careful about.

\subsection{Practical relevance}

A weather-adjusted pace prediction lets a runner see how a given day's heat is likely to change the finish time they would otherwise expect from their fitness, and lets them explore how a warmer or cooler forecast would move that expectation. The results show that the population component of this correction is ready for such use. The deep model's population heat curve is well scaled, since its calibration coefficient is compatible with one, it modestly improves held-out prediction, and it agrees with an independent coaching heuristic and with the published literature. Individualized heat correction is a harder problem. The per-runner sensitivity score carries reproducible predictive information, but on a heart-rate-aware baseline its residual direction is reversed, so it cannot yet be applied as a direct, expected-direction adjustment. The honest reading is that a population heat adjustment can be applied to finish-time predictions now, while a validated per-runner heat adjustment awaits further work.

\subsection{What would strengthen the result}

Several additions would sharpen the picture. A labeled race set is the most valuable, because the corpus is training runs and the experiment can only measure held-out prediction, not race-day calibration directly. A baseline whose mediation structure is explicit enough to interpret the personalized direction would resolve whether the reversed coefficient reflects heart rate already carrying heat sensitivity or a genuine problem with the score. Denser sampling of genuinely hot runs would tighten the hot-end estimates, which are thin by design because hot races are cancelled or postponed. A few variables the literature flags are absent from raw GPX files but would plausibly improve prediction: body mass, since heat production scales with body mass and smaller runners are advantaged in heat \citep{marino2008, maughan2010}; biological sex, an established performance differential under heat \citep{llewellyn2025, vihma2010, kelly2023, weiss2022b}; and resting heart rate, one of the most validated acclimation markers \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, pryor2019}. Lab-only variables such as plasma volume or sweat sodium concentration cannot be obtained from consumer devices and are outside the scope of this approach. Throughout, the appropriate accuracy metric is calibration and signed bias on hot runs rather than average error over the full distribution, because most days are temperate and the heat effect is small next to run-to-run variation.

\subsection{Limitations}

The headline caveat is that this is runner-disjoint held-out prediction, not a causal estimate of heat's physiological effect. The corpus is training runs, so every number describes how well one model predicts held-out pace, and the improvements are calibration and accuracy gains rather than measured heat responses. The held-out cohort also spans WBGT only to about $25\,^{\circ}$C, and the hottest bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones, so the hot-end estimates carry the most sampling uncertainty. The reported robust MAPE and bias are clipped at the 99th (and 1st) percentiles precisely because a few large-error rows would otherwise dominate.

The personalized result is real but narrow. A runner-matched deep-learning score improves held-out prediction, and a shuffled control removes the gain, but in this specification the incremental effect is mostly temperature-independent, only about 55\% of it survives when forced through the temperature curve, and its residual-layer coefficient is reversed. Whether that reversal reflects mediation through the heart-rate and pace features in the baseline, rather than a backwards physiological ordering, cannot be settled here, because the sensitivity branch that removes current effort loses the reversal but is too imprecise to be decisive. The personalized DL score should therefore be treated as predictive runner-level information, not as a validated, directly applicable heat multiplier.

The acclimation evidence is complementary and suggestive rather than confirmatory. Recent exposure predicts lower DL sensitivity and curated hot runs improve after exposure, but the data cannot validate individual adaptation magnitudes near race-relevant heat. This document will be revised as a labeled race set becomes available and as the mediation structure of the baseline is made explicit enough to interpret the personalized calibration direction.
