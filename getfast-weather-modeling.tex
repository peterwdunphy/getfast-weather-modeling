{\large\textbf{Abstract}}\\[1pt]
\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}
\end{center}

\begin{singlespace}\small

\noindent\textbf{Motivation.} Anthropogenic warming is making extreme-heat events more frequent, and across the six Abbott World Marathon Majors abnormally hot editions have arrived roughly six times more often per year since 2020 than in the preceding four decades. Finish-time predictors have improved over the same period, drawing on training data collected passively by GPS watches, but they remain weather-blind, and are therefore systematically optimistic on precisely the days when a runner most needs a dependable estimate.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Literature.} Heat slows distance runners nonlinearly, with performance flat through a cool optimum near 10--15\,\textdegree C wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and degrading increasingly above it. The cost is uneven: slower runners lose two to three times more pace per degree than elite fields, and the penalty grows with the duration of the event. Sensitivity also depends on recent exposure, since 5--14 days of heat produces adaptations that decay within weeks. Large observational studies rarely model this individual variation, while the controlled experiments that measure it directly run at 10--30 participants and seldom connect to race performance.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Methods.} We assembled roughly four hundred thousand training runs, each matched to the weather it was run in. A transformer trained on each runner's recent streams predicts pace and, through counterfactual WBGT sweeps, yields a population heat curve derived from inferred per-runner heat sensitivities. Both are added to a weather-blind pace model and evaluated on a runner-disjoint holdout of 24{,}311 activities from 154 runners, using robust error, signed bias, and a direct calibration of each feature. The race-day scale of the curve is then estimated on official results: 8.22 million finishes from 840 editions of marathons and half marathons, linked into 1.57 million repeat finishers, with each edition's weather taken from its official date, first mass-start time and start line.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results.} The learned heat response steepens above roughly 16\,\textdegree C, reaching a 4.3\% pace penalty at 28\,\textdegree C relative to 10\,\textdegree C on training runs, with the model's per-runner responses spanning 3.1\% to 5.4\% at the tenth and ninetieth percentiles. Against the official results, within-person finish times track the same curve scaled by a factor of 5.1 for the marathon (95\% interval 4.5--5.7; 321 editions of 49 races) and 4.2 for the half marathon (3.6--4.8; 239 editions of 40 races), so a marathon run at 21\,\textdegree C WBGT costs about 6.9\% of finish time, roughly fifteen minutes for a 3:30 marathoner; across ten race distances the scale rises with race duration, from 1.3 for the 5K to 5.1 for the marathon, about twice the training-run scale at matched duration; a rise in WBGT during the race and a race day hotter than the preceding 60 days add to that cost, while degree-hours above 22\,\textdegree C are not identified as a separate cost. Adding these features lowered held-out error only modestly, from 7.56 to 7.33\%. The informative result is calibration: the weather-blind model predicts runs in the hottest band 1.84\% too fast, and the heat features remove that optimism. A shuffled control confirms the personalized gain is runner-specific, but its effect is nearly independent of temperature and its direction reverses once heart rate is conditioned on.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Implications.} Existing work on weather and running performance is retrospective, explaining races already run, while predictors that forecast forward ignore conditions entirely. This study joins the two, predicting pace for a run that has not yet taken place from a runner's prior training and the conditions forecast for it. The population response is well scaled on training runs, its calibrated magnitude matching the correction an independent pace model requires, and its race-day scale is now measured rather than assumed; it is recoverable from data runners already generate. Individualized correction remains unresolved: a runner's training heat slope does not improve prediction of held-out race efforts, earlier races improve it mostly by correcting the runner's general miss against the predictor, and in official results a runner's race history measures their own heat cost with a reliability near 0.04.

\end{singlespace}

\section{Introduction}

Anthropogenic climate change has at least doubled the likelihood of record-breaking hot years globally since the late 1960s \citep{king2017}. Over the same period the share of global land area affected by moderate-to-extreme heat waves tripled, and severe heat waves are projected to recur biannually in major population centers by the century's end \citep{russo2014}.

Marathons expose tens of thousands of runners to whatever conditions race day delivers, and the six World Marathon Majors (Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago, New York City, and Tokyo) show a marked increase in the frequency of unusually hot editions. Only three of the 211 races held between 1980 and 2019 exceeded a wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) of 65 °F (18 °C), a rate of about 0.08 per year. Three of the 29 races held between 2020 and 2026 already have, a rate of roughly 0.43 per year and a sixfold increase.\footnote{WBGT was estimated using Open-Meteo reanalysis with the Stull (2011) wet-bulb formula and a globe-temperature approximation following \citet{hunter1999}. Fixing the lookup at 10\,am local gives a consistent cross-decade comparison but does not perfectly match on-course exposure, since gun times have shifted over the decades.} The Chicago Marathon is the clearest individual case, with three of its hottest editions falling in the 2020s.

Those conditions confound an already difficult prediction problem. An accurate marathon projection matters to the millions who attempt the distance each year, since it underwrites pacing, goal-setting, and training adjustment. Classic practitioner formulas, including Yasso 800s and VDOT \citep{yasso2008life, gaudette2012yasso, daniels2013running}, remain unpublished in peer-reviewed form decades after their inception. The growth of GPS watches among runners, which track their position, heart rate, and altitude consistently throughout training, offers exciting new pathways for generalizable computational models. 

What every one of these approaches shares, including the predictors now shipped with consumer running platforms \citep{garmin_race_predictor, strava_performance_predictions}, is that it is weather-blind. The literature that does quantify weather effects sits on the other side of the same gap, describing races already run rather than forecasting ones still to come. \citet{vernon2021} documented the cost directly: at the warm 2018 London Marathon (24\,\textdegree C maximum), 364 surveyed mass-participation finishers reported times roughly 47 minutes slower than they had expected, about 14\% off target. A predictor that ignores race-day conditions is therefore systematically optimistic on precisely the days when a runner most needs a dependable estimate.

This paper asks whether that missing weather term can be learned from passively collected training data. We assemble an observational corpus of roughly four hundred thousand training runs, each matched to the weather in which it was run, and train a transformer on each runner's recent training streams to predict pace. Sweeping temperature counterfactually through the trained model yields two quantities: a population heat curve, describing the pace penalty the average runner incurs as conditions warm, and a per-runner sensitivity score, placing an individual within that distribution. We then test whether either improves a weather-blind pace model on runners held out entirely from fitting, and calibrate both against the correction that model actually requires. The population curve proves well scaled and removes the systematic optimism the weather-blind model shows on hot days; the curve's shape agrees with the existing literature and is, to our knowledge, the first estimate of the pace cost of heat learned from individuals' own training records; the per-runner score is reproducibly predictive, but its effect is nearly constant across temperature and does not survive conditioning on the physiological signals the baseline already observes. Official race results are then used to measure how much steeper the same curve is on race day than on a training run.

The paper makes five contributions relative to that literature. First, the shape of the training-run heat cost is learned without a parametric form by a transformer trained on runners' own GPS training streams, and its magnitude is then estimated, not assumed, in each setting where it is applied. The shape agrees with race-result syntheses fitted with parametric, smooth and tree-based models \citep{ely2007, elhelou2012, knechtle2019, mantzios2022, yang2026}; a model of wearable workouts has learned environmental effects on heart rate \citep{nazaret2023}, but we found no earlier estimate of the pace cost of heat from individuals' training records. Second, the training-run and race-day costs are placed on one scale: across ten race distances the race-day scale rises with log race duration and is about twice the training-run scale at matched duration. The ordering by duration has been reported across elite championship events \citep{guy2014}, within marathons by finishing time \citep{ely2007, vihma2010, montain2007} and in the half marathon \citep{hodgson2022}; we found no earlier measurement of the ratio to training, which here compares a device-user cohort with official-results finishers. Third, race-day scales come from within-person edition effects of 1.57 million repeat finishers with weather over each edition's sourced race window, extending within-person designs that followed one race \citep{tan2022}, elite athletes \citep{wang2024} or repeat finishers of North American marathons with a fixed 06:00 to 14:00 air temperature \citep{oliveira2026}. Fourth, the build and decay of heat acclimation in heart rate are estimated from natural experiments in routine wearable records; the kinetics are consistent with laboratory induction and decay \citep{periard2015, flouris2014, daanen2018}. Fifth, the persistence of individual heat sensitivity is measured in training and tested against race day: runners differ persistently in their heart-rate response to heat, but neither that trait nor a runner's race history predicts an individual's race-day heat cost with useful reliability.

Section~\ref{sec:literature} synthesizes the evidence on heat effects and acclimation that motivates this design, Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data and the held-out experiment, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws out their implications and what remains open.

\section{How Heat Affects Endurance Running Performance}
\label{sec:literature}

Heat degrades endurance running performance through a chain that begins with a physical constraint on heat dissipation, passes through cardiovascular and central strain, and ends in a pacing decision. This section follows that chain, then turns to why the resulting effect is concentrated in endurance events, how it has been measured and modeled, and how it varies across runners.

Running is thermally expensive. Roughly 75\% to 80\% of metabolic energy turnover during exercise appears as heat rather than as mechanical work. That heat must either be dissipated or stored in body tissue \citep{maughan2007}. Storage raises core temperature, and the margin available is narrow, since enzymatic function is near optimal around 39\,\textdegree C while deterioration sets in above roughly 40.5\,\textdegree C \citep{martin2007}. Competitive runners operate close to that ceiling in the heat. Among finishers of the 2019 World Athletics road races, held at WBGT between 23.5 and 30.6\,\textdegree C, 16\% completed their event with a core temperature above 40\,\textdegree C, with a peak recorded value of 40.7\,\textdegree C \citep{aylwin2023}.

Heat balance is an accounting identity, and writing it out locates the constraint. The rate at which heat accumulates in body tissue is whatever metabolic production leaves once the exchange routes have acted,
\begin{equation}
  S \;=\; M \;-\; W \;-\; (R + C + K) \;-\; E,
\end{equation}
with $M$ metabolic heat production, $W$ external work, $R$, $C$ and $K$ radiative, convective and conductive exchange, and $E$ evaporative cooling. In running $W$ is small against $M$ and $K$ is negligible, so storage rises whenever production outruns the sum of the other three. Human tissue has a heat capacity near $3.47\,\mathrm{kJ}$ per \textdegree C, so a sustained imbalance translates quickly into rising core temperature \citep{maughan2010}.

The routes do not contribute equally, and their shares move with conditions. Using the coefficients in \citet{taylor2006}, where convective loss scales as $8.3\,(T_{sk}-T_a)\sqrt{v}$, radiant loss as $5.2\,(T_{sk}-T_{mrt})$ and evaporative loss as $124\,(P_{sk}-P_a)\sqrt{v}$ per square metre, a runner at $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with mean skin temperature at $31\,^{\circ}$C, the value Appendix~\ref{app:physics} adopts from field measurements in a hot championship marathon \citep{aylwin2023}, sheds roughly 85\% of its capacity evaporatively at $25\,^{\circ}$C and 50\% humidity, against 12\% by convection and 3\% by radiation. In cool conditions convection matters considerably more, near 25\% at $10\,^{\circ}$C, because the skin-to-air gradient is wide. In genuinely hot conditions the dry routes turn against the runner: at $33\,^{\circ}$C the air is two degrees warmer than the skin, and convection and radiation together add heat equal to about 9\% of what evaporation can remove. That is small next to evaporation, which is why \citet{taylor2006} can set the dry terms aside in that case.

So evaporation dominates in the conditions this paper is about, and the dry routes matter most exactly where heat is not a problem. Both dry routes reverse once air temperature exceeds skin temperature, beyond which the runner gains heat from the environment rather than losing it \citep{cheuvront2001}. With skin at $31\,^{\circ}$C that crossover sits near $31\,^{\circ}$C air temperature, several degrees below the $35\,^{\circ}$C typical of laboratory treadmill work, and in sun the radiant route reverses at a lower air temperature, because the mean radiant temperature then exceeds air temperature. Humidity acts almost entirely through the evaporative term, since it enters the vapour-pressure gradient $P_{sk}-P_a$ directly; its effect on the convective coefficient runs through air density and conductivity, which differ by around one percent between dry and saturated air, and is not material.

Evaporation is capped not by how hard a runner sweats but by how much vapour the air will accept. Modeling world-record performances at $33\,^{\circ}$C and 50\% relative humidity, \citet{taylor2006} calculated a required evaporation of about $4{,}200\,\mathrm{kJ}$ against a maximum achievable evaporation of $1{,}100\,\mathrm{kJ}$, so barely a quarter of the sweat needed for balance could evaporate at all. Sweat beyond that ceiling drips rather than cools, and is fluid lost for no thermal return. Radiation adds a load air temperature does not register: the $73\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ difference \citet{cheuvront2021} measured between early and late Boston Marathon start windows is worth roughly one metabolic equivalent of extra heat production for a four-hour finisher.

This is why the performance response to heat is a threshold rather than a gradient. While maximum evaporation exceeds what balance requires, the runner is in compensable conditions, storage stays near zero and warming costs little. Once the requirement exceeds the ceiling the conditions are uncompensable, storage is necessarily positive, and core temperature climbs until the runner slows enough to bring production down. Which regime a given day falls in depends on humidity and radiation as much as on air temperature, which is the physical reason a composite index outperforms the thermometer.

The strain this imposes is cardiovascular before it is thermal, as skin blood flow directed to the surface for cooling competes with muscle for a finite cardiac output. Dehydration compounds the problem: beyond a 2\% loss of body weight, each further 1\% is associated with roughly a 0.4\,\textdegree C rise in rectal temperature during prolonged running. Sweat rates scale with conditions as well, averaging 0.83\,L\,h$^{-1}$ in marathons below 15\,\textdegree C against 1.2\,L\,h$^{-1}$ above it \citep{cheuvront2001}.

For a competitive runner, these strains express themselves in pace. In a simulated half marathon at WBGT 25.8\,\textdegree C, running speed and oxygen uptake fell significantly once core temperature reached about 39\,\textdegree C, which occurred between 6 and 9\,km, after which core temperature plateaued for the remainder of the race \citep{rodrigues2020}. Real race data corroborate this finding. \citet{ely2008} found that the gap between a runner's closing and opening 5\,km pace widened systematically as conditions warmed, in four major marathons the ratio of second-half to first-half time rose convexly with temperature \citep{oliveira2026}, and \citet{maughan2007} report that post-marathon core temperature correlates with the time for the final 10\,km rather than with the overall finishing time. Core temperature climbs toward a ceiling over the opening miles and is then held near it by the runner conceding speed. Heat, therefore, acts less as a fixed penalty than as a constraint that tightens as the race proceeds that is paid most heavily in the closing stages. 

That accumulation logic extends across events. Comparing seven World Championships from 1999 to 2011, spanning 18.5 to 30\,\textdegree C, \citet{guy2014} found the sign of the effect reverses with distance: sprinters were substantially faster in the heat ($d = 2.4$ and $2.3$ for men at 100 and 200\,m) while the marathon was the most impaired event of all, at $-3.1\%$ for men and $-2.7\%$ for women.

Sports scientists have converged on wet-bulb globe temperature as the most accurate predictor of heat-related performance stress. The standard formula is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WBGT} = 0.7\,T_{\mathrm{nwb}} + 0.2\,T_{\mathrm{bg}} + 0.1\,T_{\mathrm{db}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{nwb}}$ is the natural wet-bulb temperature, measured by a thermometer with a wet cloth wick ventilated by ambient wind and so capturing humidity and air movement together, $T_{\mathrm{bg}}$ is the black-globe temperature, measured inside a matte-black copper sphere and so capturing radiant load, and $T_{\mathrm{db}}$ is ambient air temperature \citep{cheuvront2021}. This weighting follows the physiology of heat's effect on performance described above. It is also recoverable from the heat balance rather than merely calibrated to it. For a runner with wetted skin, the coefficients in \citet{taylor2006} imply a ratio of evaporative to convective transfer of 14.9\,\textdegree C\,kPa$^{-1}$, which is the standard psychrometric constant. Substituting the definition of the wet-bulb temperature into the sum of the dry and evaporative terms removes air temperature and humidity as separate quantities and leaves total heat-loss capacity a function of skin and wet-bulb temperature alone. Along a line of constant wet-bulb temperature the dry and evaporative shares trade off exactly while their sum does not move, so the heavy weight on the wet-bulb term reflects that it carries the information the two variables jointly hold. Appendix~\ref{app:physics} gives the derivation. The most weight corresponds to the humidity-sensitive term, as evaporative capacity is the binding constraint, while dry heat alone is comparatively benign. WBGT has been found to outperform raw air temperature in cross-event comparison \citep{mantzios2022, ely2007, aylwin2023}.  

The exact functional form of the heat penalty remains contested, and Table~\ref{tab:forms} collects those that have been published. Linear models dominate the literature and adequately describe the moderate ranges observed at most major marathons \citep{ely2007, knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019, vernon2021}.\footnote{These moderate ranges are partly an artifact of major marathons being canceled once heat passes a threshold.} A linear form cannot capture the acceleration of the penalty at the hot end, however, and several designs recover it. Quadratic fits appear across single-race and multi-race designs \citep{martin1999, elhelou2012, gasparetto2020}, while the largest synthesis is explicitly piecewise: across 1{,}258 elite races, \citet{mantzios2022} reported a flat band up to 15\,\textdegree C WBGT with performance declining by $0.4 \pm 0.4\%$ per \textdegree C above it. Table~\ref{tab:forms} collects these forms.

The estimated optimal temperature for endurance running performance is also a source of scholarly contention. Optimal air temperature estimates range from about 3.8 to 9.9\,\textdegree C \citep{elhelou2012} and 5 to 10\,\textdegree C \citep{llewellyn2025} through 10 to 12\,\textdegree C \citep{maughan2010} to 12 to 15\,\textdegree C \citep{martin1999, martin2007}. On WBGT, \citet{mantzios2022} place the marathon optimum at 7.5\,\textdegree C. \citet{gasparetto2020} locate a tipping point near 8\,\textdegree C. This wide span reflects, in part, the vast individual-level differences among runners that together constitute heat tolerance.

The most studied of these individual variations is performance level. Across nearly every large observational study, slower runners lose more pace per degree than faster ones. \citet{ely2007} found that between the coolest and warmest WBGT quartiles, the 25th, 50th, 100th and 300th finishers slowed by 2.6, 3.5, 4.9 and 7.9\% respectively. At the Boston Marathon, \citet{nikolaidis2019} estimated 1 minute 53 seconds of slowing per \textdegree C for all finishers against 38 seconds for the top ten. \citet{knechtle2021b} found that running the New York City marathon in warm temperatures added 7.73 minutes for the average finisher but only 1.87 minutes for top-ten finishers. This finding has been challenged by other studies, however, which find that faster finishes have a strong pace penalty in hot weather \citep{gasparetto2020, knechtle2021a}. In the largest within-runner analysis, of 244{,}237 repeat finishers of North American marathons, a runner one standard deviation faster lost 0.9 percentage points more above 24\,\textdegree C, while in the marathon-level analysis of the same data the winning time rose by 4.1\% against 7.5\% for the mean \citep{oliveira2026}.

Why slower runners should lose remains unsettled. The simplest explanation is exposure: a runner who is on course longer absorbs more of the day's thermal and solar load and often runs through its hottest hours. Physique supplies a second. Among marathoners with laboratory-measured fitness, percent body fat predicted how far a runner finished from their predicted time in warm conditions ($r = 0.503$) \citep{roach2012}. A third consideration corresponds to heat tolerance. Across marathon studies, faster runners generate more metabolic heat and record a hotter post-race rectal temperature \citep{cheuvront2001}. Elite runners therefore carry the larger thermal load and still lose less time to it. What separates the elite then from the mass field is not a lighter heat burden but a greater capacity to continue running at race pace while carrying it.

Other individual-level variables regarding heat sensitivity have produced mixed results. \citet{elhelou2012} found the performance gap between men and women stable across temperatures and \citet{knechtle2021b} found no significant temperature-by-sex interaction, while \citet{roach2012} and \citet{llewellyn2025} report women finishing further from their predicted times than men in warm conditions. Within runners, \citet{oliveira2026} found a smaller penalty above 24\,\textdegree C for women (7.5\%) than for men (9.4\%). For age, the largest heat effects appear in middle-aged rather than the oldest runners \citep{knechtle2021b, weiss2022a}, a pattern that plausibly reflects racing intensity rather than age itself.

Heat imposes the same thermodynamic accounting on every runner, but not the same constraint: the balance in Equation~1 holds universally while its terms do not, since sweat capacity, body surface area and the mass over which heat is stored all differ between individuals, and two runners in identical conditions can therefore sit on opposite sides of the compensable threshold (Appendix~\ref{app:physics}). The cost heat exacts consequently varies widely. Finishing position, body composition, sex, and age each capture part of that variation---each an observable correlates of a latent heat tolerance. This unobserved quality motivates our approach of inferring heat tolerance from a runner's own history of running in varied conditions rather than using demographic proxies.

\subsection{Heat Acclimation}

The differences described above are semi-sticky attributes of a runner. Performance level and body morphology shift slowly, if at all, across a training block. They describe who a runner is rather than what that runner has recently done. Heat acclimation is different in kind. It is a shorter-term physiological response to a training stimulus, acquired within days and lost within weeks. It is the lever of heat tolerance that a runner can most rapidly and deliberately change.

Repeated exposure to heat produces a coordinated set of adaptations that make the same effort thermally cheaper. Plasma volume expands by roughly 7--18\%, resting core temperature falls, sweating begins earlier and loses less sodium, and skin blood flow responds sooner, so at a fixed workload an acclimatized runner shows a lower heart rate, a lower core temperature, and a lower perceived effort together \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, racinais2017, gibson2020}. The practical consequence is an increased thermoregulatory effect that allows a runner to hold a given pace longer \citep{marino2008, rodrigues2020}. The adaptation can be induced three ways: by exercising in the heat, by passive heating such as sauna or hot-water immersion, or simply by living and training in a hot climate. All three work over a span of about 5--14 days, with hot-climate residence combined with training producing the most complete response \citep{garrett2011, periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, heathcote2018, pryor2019, schmit2017, brown2022}.

Research generally agrees on the onset of heat acclimation and its decay. Heart-rate and core-temperature adaptations develop fastest, reaching about 75\% of their eventual gain within 4--7 days; plasma volume expands over 3--6 days; sweat-rate adaptations continue to develop through 10--14 days \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, racinais2020, chalmers2014, cubel2024}. Decay runs on a similar order once exposure stops, at roughly 2.5\% per day for the heart-rate channel, though re-acclimation is considerably faster than initial induction \citep{daanen2018, racinais2020, pryor2019}. A block of 10--14 days therefore captures most of the available adaptation, and a single session every 5--7 days maintains it \citep{daanen2018, garrett2011}. A runner's acclimation state can accordingly be approximated from training-temperature history over the trailing two to four weeks, weighting recent sessions more heavily. Across 29 studies \citet{daanen2018} fit heart-rate decay as $3.6 + 2.3$ per day without heat exposure and core-temperature decay at $2.6$ per day, both in percent of the adaptation gained. Sweat rate behaves differently, since its decay equation carries no term for elapsed days and depends instead on the length and intensity of the original acclimation block, so a longer or hotter block yields a more durable adaptation.

Most large-scale observational studies handle heat adaptation through geographic or fitness proxies such as climate of origin or tropical residence, and almost none fit an individual-level acclimation state \citep{ioannou2018, scheer2021, knechtle2021a, tan2022}. The exception is \citet{vernon2021}, who computed the difference between race-day maximum temperature and each runner's rolling 60-day prior training-environment temperature and reported a correlation of $r = 0.85$ between that differential and mean finish time, with about 2.6\% additional slowdown per 5 °C of positive differential. \citet{oliveira2026} tested heat accumulated in a runner's earlier races, in total and over the preceding year, and found no reduction in the penalty; their interval excluded an offset larger than about 1.5\% of it per standard deviation of prior exposure.

Heat acclimation effects are well established in experimental settings. The largest meta-analysis, pooling 1{,}531 participants across controlled trials, reports that acclimation improves subsequent exercise performance and capacity in the heat (Hedges' $g = 0.7$) while lowering resting core temperature ($g = -0.6$) and heart rate ($g = -0.5$) and raising sweat rate ($g = 0.4$) \citep{tyler2024}. Trials in runners recover the same result. \citet{james2017} assigned 17 runners to either short-term heat acclimation or work-matched training in cool conditions, and found the acclimated group improved 5\,km time-trial performance in the heat by 6.2\% against 0.6\% for controls ($p = 0.029$), alongside a 20.8\% gain in total run distance. 

Sustained over years rather than weeks, this dynamic process hardens into something closer to a fixed trait. A runner who lives and trains in a hot climate carries an adaptation that never fully decays, because the exposure never stops. At the Marathon des Sables, a seven-day ultramarathon in the Sahara Desert, athletes from warmer countries ran 10.7\% faster overall than those from colder countries \citep{ioannou2018}. The advantage does not scale indefinitely, however: athletes from the hottest countries of origin, at 25 to 35\,\textdegree C, gained nothing over those from moderately warm countries at 15 to 25\,\textdegree C. Whether that ceiling reflects a limit on useful adaptation or the confounding of origin climate with training resources cannot be settled from the design, but it is consistent with the induction literature above, in which most of the available adaptation arrives well short of the most extreme exposure. Habitual climate, then, is a slow-moving average of exposure, and race-day performance turns on the deviation from it. Measuring that deviation requires knowing the conditions a runner has actually trained in, one session at a time.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Design}

The study estimates whether a heat response learned by a deep-learning model improves pace prediction for runners the model has never seen. A weather-blind pace model is fitted first, and heat information is then supplied to it in two stages: a population heat curve that depends only on the conditions of a run, and an individualized, per-runner sensitivity score. Each stage is evaluated on the same runner-disjoint holdout against negative and constrained controls and is separately calibrated against the correction the weather-blind model actually requires. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the design.

\subsection{Data}

The corpus consists of outdoor running activities recorded on wrist wearable devices by athletes who consented to the research use of their data. Activities were kept only if pace, WBGT and every baseline feature were present. Mean heart rate had to lie above 60 and below 210\,bpm and mean pace above 2.5 and below 11.5\,min\,km$^{-1}$, which removes sensor dropouts and GPS artifacts without reference to the weather. Runs above 28\,\textdegree C WBGT were excluded, since the corpus thins sharply beyond that point (Appendix~\ref{app:ceiling}). The resulting frame holds 403{,}719 activities from 2{,}420 runners across roughly one hundred countries. This is an enlarged corpus relative to an earlier round of this study: weather enrichment was extended to activities recorded after the original cutoff and to runners who later backfilled their histories, which raised the number of runs above 28\,\textdegree C WBGT from 1{,}846 to 4{,}205 and made the 28\,\textdegree C ceiling supportable.

The target variable, $y_{a,i}$, is the mean pace of activity $i$ for runner $a$, in minutes per kilometer. Runners were split by identity rather than by activity, so no runner contributes to both fitting and evaluation. One runner in ten was held out, leaving 24{,}311 activities from 154 runners for evaluation once the prior-history requirement described below is applied.

\subsection{Weather enrichment}

Weather was sampled from Open-Meteo along each run: once at the start, once at the finish, and every 15 minutes in between, at the runner's location. Each sample gives an outdoor WBGT that accounts for measured solar radiation and wind alongside air temperature and humidity, so that sun and airflow are represented rather than assumed. Each run is then summarized by $\bar{W}_{a,i}$, the time-weighted mean of those samples, rather than by its starting value. Thermal strain accumulates over a run, so a morning start in cool air that finishes in midday heat is described by neither endpoint alone.

\subsection{Deep-learning heat model}

Heat features come from a transformer trained on each runner's recent training streams, a factored-attention network adapted from an existing fitness-prediction architecture. Its input is a window of the 30 most recent activities, each resampled to 128 points and represented by eleven channels: date, latitude, altitude, cadence, cumulative elevation, distance, heart rate, power, activity type, outdoor WBGT, and pace. Each channel is tokenized and embedded separately; the embeddings are summed with a $1/\sqrt{C}$ scale and layer-normalized, and the result passes through attention that mixes information both within an activity and across the runner's recent activities. The model predicts pace directly. It was trained for five million optimizer steps on the fitting runners: an initial one-million-step run, resumed from its final state with the learning-rate decay stretched so that the rate reached its floor at the five-million-step mark rather than at 2.2 million. The checkpoint with the lowest held-out error, at step 4.33 million, is the one used throughout. Two heat artifacts are then read off the trained model by counterfactual WBGT sweeps that hold every other channel fixed.

The \emph{population heat curve} gives the average pace penalty as a function of conditions alone. Each fitting runner's swept response is anchored at $10\,^{\circ}$C and divided by that runner's own value at $28\,^{\circ}$C, isolating the shape of the response from its size. For the held-out comparison the median of these shapes is taken across the 1{,}786 fitting runners with a positive heat response, then rescaled by the median sensitivity of a 1{,}093-runner reference sample. The curve drawn in the figures and used for the race-day scale applies the same construction to all 1{,}961 runners with a positive response (reference sample 1{,}201), holdout runners included, since no held-out pace outcome is evaluated against it. The model is given no monotonicity constraint, so the shape of the response is whatever the network produces; a monotone projection is applied to the median shape when the published curve is assembled, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports how much it changes. All reported curves are truncated at 28\,\textdegree C, a limit set by data support rather than by the model; Appendix~\ref{app:ceiling} gives the counts, and Appendix~\ref{app:curve} gives the construction in full.

The \emph{per-runner sensitivity score} is each runner's percentile rank of DL-predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C relative to $10\,^{\circ}$C, $\mathrm{slowdown}_{28} = 100\,(\mathrm{DL}_{28^\circ}/\mathrm{DL}_{10^\circ} - 1)$. The primary, prospective version uses a strict prior of exactly 15 preceding windows and excludes the target activity, so it is fully prospective.

\subsection{Baseline model and feature progression}

The baseline is a weather-blind XGBoost pace model with no temperature input: current-run average heart rate, trailing 30-day fitted pace, current-run distance, and elevation (gain and grade). The experiment adds the deep-learning heat information in two steps. The population step adds the DL population curve's value at the run's WBGT as one feature, which the tree model is free to use or ignore. The personalized step additionally adds the runner's sensitivity score. Two controls test what the personalized step is capturing. A shuffled control reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, so any genuine personalization gain should vanish. A constrained variant supplies only the percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, which forces personalization to act through the temperature curve rather than as a free runner-level number. A supplementary branch (Section~\ref{sec:results}) replaces the heart-rate-and-trailing-pace baseline with the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, to test whether the findings survive a deliberately different, pre-run baseline.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

All comparisons use the same 24{,}311-activity holdout, identical hyperparameters, and three random seeds (100, 200, 300). Diagnostics computed on the fitting runners use five-fold cross-validation with folds formed by runner.

Two error measures are reported. Let $r_{a,i} = (y_{a,i} - \hat{y}_{a,i})/y_{a,i}$ denote the relative error of a prediction, signed so that a positive value means the run was predicted faster than it was actually run. Accuracy is summarized by a robust mean absolute percentage error,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum_{a,i} \min\!\left(|r_{a,i}|,\; q_{99}\right),
\end{equation}
in which $q_{99}$ is the 99th percentile of the absolute relative error, and calibration by the signed bias, the mean of $100\,r_{a,i}$ with the same trimming applied at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Trimming is applied identically to every model. Both measures are needed because they answer different questions: MAPE describes the typical size of an error, whereas bias describes whether a model is systematically optimistic, which is the failure mode that matters on a hot race day. Because activities from the same runner are not independent, all confidence intervals come from a bootstrap resampling runners rather than activities.

Prediction error alone cannot establish whether a heat feature points in the physiologically expected direction, since a flexible tree model can exploit a feature of either sign. We therefore fit a direct affine calibration of the two heat artifacts against the correction the weather-blind prediction requires. The calibration is estimated out-of-fold on the fitting runners, a pool of 227{,}908 activities from 1{,}413 runners, and then frozen before being applied to the holdout, so that no held-out observation informs the coefficients it is later used to judge. The form is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = \hat{p}_0\left(1 + \beta + \alpha\,c_{\mathrm{pop}} + \gamma\,d_{\mathrm{pers}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_0$ is the weather-blind prediction, $c_{\mathrm{pop}}$ the population curve evaluated at the run's WBGT, and $d_{\mathrm{pers}}$ the personalized deviation. Two coefficients carry the interpretation: $\alpha$ is the scale of the population curve, with $\alpha \approx 1$ indicating that the curve is already of the right magnitude, and $\gamma$ is the direction of the personalized deviation, with $\gamma > 0$ indicating that a runner assigned a larger predicted slowdown does in fact require a slower prediction.

\subsection{Race-day validation data}
\label{sec:raceday_methods}

The corpus is training runs, so the curve it yields is a training-run curve. To measure its scale on race day, official finish lists were assembled from a public results service for marathons and half marathons held between 2014 and 2026, used only as training data and never republished: 8{,}221{,}398 finishes across 840 editions after same-edition name collisions were removed (below). Finishers were linked into persons across editions and courses on normalised name, sex and a birth-year interval (published ages are exact for some races and age groups for others), with hometown as a tie-breaker; same-edition collisions the tie-breaker cannot separate are dropped. The linkage was checked against the service's persistent member identifier where one is present: among persons with two or more identified finishes on a course, the share for whom the identifier disagrees is an upper bound on the false-link rate. Within-person edition effects were then estimated per course from the same-course repeat pairs, $\log(t_b/t_a) = e_b - e_a + \text{age term}$, in log units, with person-clustered standard errors and the effects centred within course; editions supported by fewer than 30 repeat pairs are dropped. The first stage carries no separate years-apart term: on every course such a term is an exact linear combination of the edition contrasts, so it cannot be estimated alongside them. The common within-person change across calendar years (the ageing of repeat finishers and any change in shoes or fields) therefore stays in the edition effects and is removed in the second stage by calendar-year fixed effects; a variant with a course-specific linear trend makes the scale independent of how any first stage divides trend from edition effects.

Race-day conditions are built from official information for each edition. The race day is taken from the race's own published schedule (the race website, an archived copy of it, or the organiser's release) and failing that from a news report; where neither exists, the listed date is kept only if it falls on the weekday used by the race's confirmed editions (at least two confirmed editions, at least 80\% of them on one weekday). The start time is that of the first mass-start wave (elite, wheelchair, adaptive, relay and walking waves are not counted), from the official race page for that edition, current or archived, or from a news report. The start line is the named start location of that edition, entered per race and per year where the start moved. Weather is read from the same reanalysis as the corpus at the start line (at the nearest already extracted grid point when one lies within 5\,km, the reanalysis grid being 0.25\textdegree), interpolated linearly between hourly values, and summarised over the window that runs from the first mass start to that start plus the edition's median finish time among linked finishers; the edition's conditions are the mean outdoor WBGT over that window, from the same WBGT routine as the corpus. An edition is excluded when it was virtual, cancelled or not held, when it fell in 2020, when the results list records only a multi-day race weekend rather than the day of the race, when its date cannot be verified, when the results course is ambiguous between two races, or when no first mass-start time could be sourced from an official page or a news report. Editions whose start time is known only from a secondary source are excluded from the main estimates and enter one sensitivity estimate. Official results of shorter road races (5K, 4 and 5 miles, 8K, 10K, 12K, 15K and 10 miles), with the half marathon and marathon as a check, come from a second results service (RunSignup, United States, 2012 onward), which dates each distance separately. Editions are excluded in 2020, when virtual, on trails, with fewer than 30 finishers or without a scheduled start time; each course keeps editions timed on one basis (chip or gun). Finishers are linked within a course on name, sex and birth-year interval; the first stage, the outlier rule and the second stage are those of the main analysis; the weather window runs from the scheduled start for the edition's median finish time at the reanalysis cell of the race city; and intervals are clustered by course. The scales by distance are related to race duration by a weighted regression of $\alpha$ on the log of each band's median finish time and compared with the training-run scale at the same expected duration.

The race-day scale is the coefficient $\alpha$ in a weighted regression of the edition effects on the learned curve evaluated at the edition's WBGT, with course and calendar-year fixed effects and weights inverse to the squared standard errors; the curve enters on the same log scale as the edition effects, $100\log(1 + c/100)$. Fit is summarised by the partial $R^2$ of the curve, the share of the weighted variance left by the fixed effects that the curve explains. Before any weather enters, an edition is removed when its effect departs from a leave-one-out prediction from course and calendar-year effects alone by more than four robust standard deviations and six percentage points and the departure has a mechanical cause (a shifted or shortened course, or misrecorded times); editions affected by heat, such as a race stopped or started early, are kept. It is fitted separately for marathons and half marathons, and jointly with a distance-band interaction on $\alpha$ to test the difference between them. The same regression is reported for the subset of five marathons (New York City, Chicago, London, Boston and Big Sur, 2015--2025) on which the scale was first estimated. To separate a change in measurement from a change in sample, the regressions are repeated with the weather table these inputs replace (listed date, city geocode, and a fixed clock window beginning at 07:00 outside the five courses), restricted to the same editions. Whether the scale depends on a few runners who slowed sharply is checked by refitting it on edition quantiles of runner deviations (each finisher's log time relative to their other finishes on the course), and whether it varies with finish time by fitting it within finish-time tiers, with each runner's tier assigned from their other finishes so that the tier is not built from the outcome. The edition table is also used to ask whether any weather component adds to the curve: by a LASSO over ten race-day components (the spread and the rise of WBGT within the window, relative humidity, dew point, wind speed, gusts, solar radiation, cloud cover, precipitation and the positive deviation of Section~\ref{sec:accl_methods}) with the curve and the fixed effects left unpenalised, the penalty chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation and selection frequencies taken over 200 bootstrap resamples; and by adding to the regression two time-resolved terms computed from the hourly WBGT trajectory over the same window, the degree-hours of WBGT above 22\,\textdegree C and the rise in WBGT from the start to the end of the window. A physics-form wind-drag term (Section~\ref{sec:future}) is added to the same regression on all courses, from hourly wind over the race window reduced to runner height, the edition's median finisher speed, and course bearings taken as uniformly spread round the compass (exact for a loop); courses run point to point are dropped in a sensitivity fit. Course effects come from same-person pairs of finishes on two different courses of the same distance no more than two years apart: each pair's log time ratio, net of the calendar-year effects and of $\alpha$ times the difference in the two editions' curve values, is regressed on the course contrast, with person-clustered standard errors. Official intermediate splits, where published, give each finisher's second-half minus first-half relative pace; across editions of the same course, the change in that difference is compared with the predictions of rules that distribute an edition's heat cost over the race (uniform, instantaneous in the WBGT at each point, heat multiplied by position in the race, a storage bank, and a fade-shaped rule), scored by root-mean-square error.

\subsection{Acclimation designs}
\label{sec:accl_methods}

Acclimation is tested on three channels. On heart rate, a within-runner model of mean heart rate on log pace, distance, elevation gain per kilometre and a two-hinge WBGT basis (knots at 15 and 22\,\textdegree C) gives the heat cost in beats per minute per degree at matched load; its residual is the heart-rate excess on a run beyond what pace, terrain and the day's heat explain. Two designs then ask whether that cost depends on recent exposure. The seasonal design counts each runner's runs above 18\,\textdegree C WBGT in the preceding 21 and 60 days and the days since their last such run, and interacts the hinge terms with the log count and with an indicator for the first hot run after 45 or more days without one, with the runner's total run count over the same window as a control for training volume. The kinetics of acclimation are read from the heart-rate excess: a run's heart rate minus a within-person load model fitted on cool runs (log pace, log duration, climb), the start-hour effect and the population heat cost, on runs of at least 20 minutes; a hot run lasts at least 20 minutes at WBGT 22\,\textdegree C or above. Three natural experiments use it. Onset: the first hot-run day after 45 or more days without one, with the excess on hot runs modelled by the number of earlier hot-run days (episode fixed effects, a days-since-onset trend and a control for a hot run the day before) and compared, by person bootstrap, with calendar days and with ambient heat at the runner's location. Trips: runs more than 500\,km from a rolling home (the median location within 60 days), classed as hot (at least 5\,\textdegree C warmer than nearby home runs), neutral (within 2\,\textdegree C) or cool (at least 5\,\textdegree C cooler), with the excess relative to the runner's home runs in the 45 preceding days regressed on days since arrival with trip fixed effects. Breaks: after at least four hot-run days in 14, the first hot run back is compared with the last week of the block, by length of break. Exponential fits give half-lives, and a recursive state (gain proportional to the remaining capacity, first-order decay) is fitted by profile likelihood, with a placebo that builds the same state from heat after the run. On race results, the edition regression of Section~\ref{sec:raceday_methods} is extended with the positive part of the edition's deviation, race-window WBGT minus the mean WBGT over the same local clock window on the 60 days before the race at the start line. A third design, on the same results, locates each finisher's hometown from the published locality, geocodes it to a one-degree cell (cells with at least 30 persons are kept), and computes a decay-weighted (14-day half-life) home WBGT exposure over the 60 days before each race and the home annual mean, each expressed relative to the 60-day pre-race value of the same index at the course. Log finish time is then regressed on the interaction of the learned curve at the edition's WBGT with the recent home climate, with person and edition fixed effects and person-clustered standard errors, for all editions and separately for spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) editions; a second model adds the interaction with the annual home climate.

\subsection{Training-run scale, per-runner heat response and race efforts}
\label{sec:perrunner_methods}

\emph{Training-run scale by expected duration.} The curve's scale on training runs is estimated within runner in bands of expected duration, the run's distance multiplied by the runner's trailing 30-day fitted pace, so that heat cannot lengthen the duration that defines a band: under 30, 30 to 60, 60 to 90, 90 to 150 and over 150 minutes. Log pace is regressed on the curve at the run's WBGT with runner-by-band fixed effects and without a heart-rate control, so the coefficient is the total effect of heat on pace, after every race-distance activity is removed; a continuous version interacts the curve with log expected duration. A placebo gives every band one common scale and checks that the bands recover it. Each race-day scale is then divided by the training scale of the band matching the event's duration (90 to 150 minutes for the half marathon, over 150 for the marathon).

\emph{Persistent and recent components of the heart-rate heat slope.} Accounts that belong to one person are merged first (59 Garmin accounts span 134 user identifiers). Mean heart rate is regressed on a person-by-calendar-quarter intercept, load controls (speed relative to the trailing 30-day fitted pace, log fitted pace, 30-day volume, duration, climb and start hour), a common hinge in WBGT above 24\,\textdegree C and a person-specific slope on WBGT above 15\,\textdegree C; the same model with log pace as the outcome and heart rate relative to maximum as a control gives the pace axis. Runs are serially correlated, so the between-person variance of the slope is estimated as the precision-weighted covariance of two estimates of each person's slope from disjoint halves of their runs: alternate calendar weeks (the same seasons in both halves), early against late (split at the median date of the person's runs above 15\,\textdegree C, so only what persists across time), and odd against even heat seasons. Persons need at least ten runs above 15\,\textdegree C. Recent exposure enters as a state computed from earlier runs only, with its main effect and its interaction with the heat slope: a heat-exposure score accumulating minutes above 22\,\textdegree C WBGT with gain and decay over days, the hours run at or above 24\,\textdegree C in the preceding 28 days, and the count of runs above 18\,\textdegree C in the preceding 21 days, the last also entered for the 21 days after the run as a placebo. The share of the persistent variance explained by body-size, age, fitness and volume proxies is cross-fitted over five folds of persons. Intervals resample persons.

\emph{Heat-response groups.} Runners are placed in groups by the persistent part of their heart-rate slope, shrunk toward the population mean in proportion to its reliability, with boundaries at equal-probability quantiles of the estimated distribution of true slopes. Groups are set on the first half of each runner's history and evaluated on the second half, and each runner's posterior probability of belonging to their assigned group measures how many groups the data resolve.

\emph{Race efforts.} Race-distance efforts come from the runners' own records: marathon- and half-distance hard efforts, with loss measured against the production race predictor in points of log finish time and the typical heat cost taken as the population heat cost applied at that distance and WBGT. Every input is dated before the effort. Loss is modelled by a linear mixed model with two effects per runner, a personal miss against the prediction that applies at every temperature and a heat trait that multiplies the typical cost, with the heat-exposure score and 28-day training volume in the heat term and an optional prior for the trait from training runs (heart-rate heat slope and cold response). It is fitted by restricted maximum likelihood on four of five folds formed by runner, and each held-out effort's runner effects come from that runner's earlier efforts only; the correlation between the two runner effects was fixed at zero after the pre-specified fit was inspected. Pre-specified comparisons add earlier efforts, the training prior and the exposure score to a population model, judged by the mean absolute error of predicted loss on hot efforts (WBGT 18\,\textdegree C or more) with runner-bootstrap intervals, and groups are thirds of the posterior heat trait.

\emph{Race-to-race persistence in official results.} Whether a runner's own race-day heat cost persists is tested on repeat marathon and half-marathon finishers in the edition table: log finish time is modelled as a person effect plus an edition effect plus a person-specific slope on the curve at the edition's WBGT, so the slope is in units of the scale $\alpha$, relative to the field. Each runner's editions are split into two halves that share no course, each with at least one warm (WBGT 15\,\textdegree C or more) and one cool edition; within each half the slope is estimated on the edition's curve value minus its course mean, so that only that year's weather at the course counts, with a linear year term for personal trends. The covariance of the two halves' slopes, weighted by the product of their heat contrasts, estimates the persistent between-runner variance $\tau^2$; intervals come from a delete-one-course jackknife, a placebo shuffles which year was hot within each course, and a random-slope model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood on all repeat finishers serves as a check. The reliability of a runner's race-history estimate follows from $\tau^2$ and each runner's sampling variance.

\emph{Heart rate, cadence and stride length.} Runs of at least 3\,km with a mean pace of 3 to 12\,min/km and a device mean cadence of 130 to 215 steps per minute are analyzed within runner, with WBGT in 2\,\textdegree C bins (reference 8 to 10\,\textdegree C), log distance, climb per kilometer and log 30-day pace as covariates, and standard errors clustered by runner. Four outcomes are fitted: log pace; log pace with log heart rate added, which gives the pace difference at matched heart rate; heart rate in beats per minute with log pace added, which gives the heart-rate difference at matched pace; and log cadence with log pace added. Stride length is speed divided by cadence, so the WBGT coefficients for log cadence and log stride length, fitted without the pace term, sum to those for log speed. The pace model is refitted in three bands of the run's mean heart rate (below 75\%, 75 to 85\%, and 85\% or more of the runner's maximum). Because heat raises heart rate, two further band definitions are used: heart rate less the average within-runner heart-rate difference of the run's WBGT bin at matched pace, and thirds of each runner's own heart rate among that runner's runs in the same 4\,\textdegree C WBGT band (cells of at least six runs). Heart rate and cadence, as residuals within runner from log distance and climb, are tabulated against speed relative to the runner's 30-day pace in five WBGT bands (cells of at least 300 runs), and again against speed relative to the runner's mean pace over the whole record. Change within a run is the second half minus the first half of heart rate, speed and cadence, from 60-second segments between minute 10 and 3 minutes before the end, in a sample of up to 25 runs at WBGT 20\,\textdegree C or above and 25 below 15\,\textdegree C per runner, each 40 to 150 minutes long; it is regressed within runner on WBGT bins (reference 10 to 15\,\textdegree C) with the between-half differences in climb and net elevation and log duration. On race-distance efforts, the loss, log speed, log cadence, log stride length and the within-effort cadence change are regressed within runner on the typical heat cost with a marathon indicator (runner bootstrap), and the heat slope of the loss is re-estimated with the effort's log cadence added. At WBGT 18\,\textdegree C or above, the ratio of the heat cost to the typical cost is compared between efforts whose cadence holds within 1\% and efforts in which it falls, under three references: the change within the effort, the runner's own cool efforts (below 15\,\textdegree C), and the runner's training cadence at the same speed over the preceding 365 days (at least 10 runs).

\emph{Cadence relative to speed, heart-rate drift and decoupling.} As a reference for the cadence results, the share of a speed difference carried by cadence is estimated on cool training runs (WBGT 5 to 15\,\textdegree C) by regressing log cadence on log speed within runner with log distance and climb per kilometer (log 30-day pace added in a sensitivity fit), overall and within thirds of each runner's own speeds (runners with at least 30 cool runs); applied to the speed difference of a WBGT bin, the share gives the cadence difference expected from the change in speed alone. Within runs, heart-rate drift is the second-half minus first-half heart rate in beats per minute, in percent of first-half heart rate (taken as the run mean less half the drift) and per hour between the half midpoints. Drift at an unchanged speed is the band mean less the within-runner slope of drift on the between-half speed change times the band's mean speed change, and steady runs (45 to 90 minutes, speed change under 2\%, climb difference under 5\,m/km) are tabulated separately. Decoupling is heart-rate drift in percent minus the speed change in percent, the logarithmic form of the ratio of internal to external load used in marathon studies \citep{smyth2022}; the share of runs above 5\% is tabulated, and within-runner differences from 10 to 15\,\textdegree C are estimated with the covariates of the within-run model. The within-run cadence and heart-rate regressions are refitted with the between-half speed change added (linearly, quadratically, and with a separate slope at WBGT 20\,\textdegree C or above). Slopes per degree of WBGT above 15\,\textdegree C for heart rate and cadence at matched pace come from hinge terms at 15\,\textdegree C with log pace and the covariates of the WBGT-bin models. On race-distance efforts with a between-half climb difference of at most 30\,m/km and halves split by distance, second-half slowing (100 times the log of second-half over first-half time), heart-rate drift, decoupling and the cadence change are regressed within runner on six WBGT bands (reference 10 to 15\,\textdegree C) with a marathon indicator and the climb difference, and the cadence change is refitted with second-half slowing added. The comparison of the heat-cost ratio between efforts whose cadence holds and falls is repeated within thirds of second-half slowing, with the cadence change measured net of the slowing expected from the cool-effort relation (linear, and quadratic with climb), in a model of the loss with both terms, and with each group's mean loss on cool efforts (below 15\,\textdegree C) subtracted; the loss gap between the two groups is compared between hot and cool efforts. Intervals come from runner-clustered standard errors, except for the heat-cost ratios and the loss gaps, which use 1{,}000 runner bootstraps.

\emph{Training and race-day cost within the corpus.} To compare training and race-day cost in the same runners, log finish time of race-distance efforts is regressed on the curve with runner-by-distance intercepts (runners with at least two efforts at the distance; clustered by runner), which is the design used for official results and involves no fitness prediction. Sensitivity fits add the fitness prediction or a time trend per runner. The difference between the official race-day scale and the scale of all training runs is divided into three additive steps: from all training runs to training runs in the duration band of the race distance, from that band to the same runners' race-distance efforts, and from those efforts to official finishers. The part corresponding to the difference in heat-exposure score between hot efforts and hot training runs of the band (WBGT 18\,\textdegree C or above) is computed separately, from a band model with the score as a level term and as an interaction with the curve. Efforts with a held-out individual multiplier from the mixed model are divided into fifths of that multiplier (cut points from efforts at 18\,\textdegree C or above) and the scale on the curve is estimated in each fifth. Efforts on the date, distance and start line of an official edition are assigned to that edition without the use of athlete identity, and their loss is regressed on the edition's official effect with a marathon indicator, clustered by edition. The mixed model's proportional term for the heat-exposure score is compared with a model that adds level terms for the score and for volume, and the score is related, within runner and distance and with the curve in the model, to the fitness prediction, the finish time and the loss separately.

\subsection{The heat response the model learned}

Sweeping temperature through the trained model, holding everything else fixed, traces the pace penalty attributed to heat (Figure~\ref{fig:heat_cost}). The curve is flat through the cool range, turns upward near $16\,^{\circ}$C, and steepens thereafter, reaching 1.0\% at $20\,^{\circ}$C, 2.4\% at $24\,^{\circ}$C and 4.3\% at $28\,^{\circ}$C relative to a $10\,^{\circ}$C reference. These are training-run magnitudes. Expressed as time for a runner whose cool-weather marathon is 3:30 they correspond to roughly two, five and nine minutes, and for a four-hour runner to roughly two and a half, six and ten minutes, if the race were run at training-run sensitivity; Section~\ref{sec:raceday} shows that race-day finish times follow the same curve at about five times that scale. Because the penalty is proportional to time on course, the same conditions cost a slower runner more absolute time, as Figure~\ref{fig:heat_nomogram} sets out across the range of finish times. Held-out error in the hot strata stopped improving between two and two and a half million optimizer steps while overall error continued to fall slowly. The monotone projection applied when the published curve is assembled is immaterial: it alters 4 of 37 grid points, by at most 0.05 percentage points of pace against a 4.3-point range of the median shape. Every individual runner's estimated curve decreases somewhere, yet their median is already non-decreasing to within that tolerance, so the monotone shape is a property the model recovered rather than one assumed.

Individual runners depart from that average substantially. The tenth and ninetieth percentiles of the per-runner response at $28\,^{\circ}$C are 3.1\% and 5.4\%. At training-run scale, for the 3:30 marathoner that is a gap of six and a half minutes versus eleven, and for the four-hour runner seven against thirteen, so runners at the two ends of the model's per-runner spread differ by five to six minutes in identical conditions. That spread is the model's, not a validated individual heat tolerance: Section~\ref{sec:results} shows the per-runner score carries runner-level information that is not specific to heat.

Because the curve is estimated without reference to any published result, it can be placed against the existing literature directly (Figure~\ref{fig:heateffects}). The published forms fall into two bands: steep slopes for amateur and mass fields, reaching 10 to 16\% by $30\,^{\circ}$C \citep{ely2007, nikolaidis2019, knechtle2019, vernon2021}, and shallow slopes for elite fields, near 3 to 5\% \citep{mantzios2022, martin1999, guy2014}. The curve estimated here sits at the lower edge of that range. While our model is trained on amateur and mass fields, it also uses training runs, not solely labeled races, and Section~\ref{sec:raceday} shows that this accounts for the lower penalty: on official race results the same curve applies at about five times the training-run scale.

The shape the model settles on is the one the physiology predicts, and it is a shape neither of the two dominant published forms can produce. Below roughly $16\,^{\circ}$C the curve is nearly flat: evaporative capacity exceeds what the runner needs to shed, heat balance holds, and warming costs almost nothing. Above it the curve steepens as the environment's capacity to remove heat falls below the rate of production, so storage accumulates and pace must be conceded. A threshold followed by acceleration is exactly the signature of a constraint that is slack and then binds.

The curvature has a specific physical source, and it is worth stating because it predicts a shape independently of anything the model was shown. The quantity a runner is graded against is the ratio of the evaporation required for heat balance to the maximum the environment will accept, which \citet{taylor2006} term thermal compensability. Its numerator rises close to linearly as conditions warm, because dry exchange is proportional to the skin-to-air gradient and simply reverses sign once air passes skin temperature, near $31\,^{\circ}$C. Its denominator falls, and falls at an accelerating rate, because saturation vapor pressure is convex in temperature: it gains about 6\% of its own value per degree (6.6\% at $12\,^{\circ}$C, 5.9\% at $26\,^{\circ}$C) on a base that more than doubles over that span, so the evaporative ceiling sheds about 22\,W\,m$^{-2}$ per degree at a wet-bulb temperature of $12\,^{\circ}$C but about 46 at $26\,^{\circ}$C. A near-linear numerator over a concave denominator is convex, so the physiologically binding quantity must accelerate even though neither of its parts does. For a 70\,kg runner at three-and-a-half-hour marathon effort, in air at 60\% relative humidity under $200\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ of solar load, the required fraction of maximal evaporation rises from 0.35 at a WBGT of $16\,^{\circ}$C to 0.72 at $24\,^{\circ}$C and passes 1, the limit of compensability, near $27\,^{\circ}$C. Its increase per degree grows more than fourfold between 16 and $28\,^{\circ}$C, from 0.03 to 0.14. \citet{taylor2006} further note that strain rises gradually as the ratio approaches unity rather than only at the point where it is reached, which matters here: a runner who held pace until the hard limit bound would show almost no penalty and then a collapse, which is not the behavior any measured curve displays.

That argument constrains the shape of the response and not its size, so it can be checked against the learned curve without either one being used to fit the other. Between the 20 to $24\,^{\circ}$C and 24 to $28\,^{\circ}$C bands the learned curve steepens from 0.33 to 0.48\% per degree, a factor of 1.45. The compensability ratio steepens by a factor of 1.80 over the same interval at the reference conditions, and between 1.5 and 2.7 across the humidities and solar loads considered in Appendix~\ref{app:physics}. The two agree in sign and are close in size, with the measured curvature falling just below the range the physics implies. A gap in that direction is consistent with the selection mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:selection}, all of which act to understate the penalty on the hottest days, and it leaves room for the additional convexity that \citet{taylor2006} attribute to sweat that drips rather than evaporates once the requirement approaches the ceiling. The physics predicts that the curve should bend and roughly how sharply, not that the penalty should reach 4.3\% at $28\,^{\circ}$C. Appendix~\ref{app:physics} gives the calculation.

A linear form cannot represent that threshold at all. It must either overstate the penalty in mild conditions or understate it in hot ones, and the published linear fits do the former, which is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:heateffects} as slopes that rise steadily from $10\,^{\circ}$C where the measured response is flat. A quadratic can bend, but it bends without limit. Fitted over the 8 to $20\,^{\circ}$C range their data spans, the cluster-specific quadratics of \citet{gasparetto2020} already reach 16 and 21\% at $20\,^{\circ}$C for their two fastest clusters, and extrapolating them to $28\,^{\circ}$C would imply slowdowns near 50\%. No runner in the corpus behaves that way. The divergence is a property of the functional form rather than of the data it was fitted to, which is why those curves are drawn here only over the range in which they were estimated.

The learned curve avoids both failures because nothing constrains it to a polynomial. It is flat where the constraint is slack and steepens where the constraint binds, without a polynomial's unbounded growth: above the reported range the raw sweep slows again (Appendix~\ref{app:ceiling}), though that region is too sparsely observed to interpret.

Grouping runs by heart rate makes the heat slope look effort-dependent, but heart rate is not a clean measure of effort here, because heat itself raises it and so moves hot runs into the higher groups. Across pooled quintiles of mean heart rate the slope roughly doubles from the easiest fifth to the hardest (Figure~\ref{fig:hrinteraction}); with fifths formed within each runner the factor is about 1.45; and with effort labels fixed before the run (the effort a runner usually runs on that weekday, from the previous 56 days), hard days minus easy days is $+0.02\%$ per \textdegree C [$-0.03$, $+0.07$] on a base of $0.20\%$ (266{,}995 runs, 1{,}357 runners). Within a runner, the planned effort of a training run changes the pace cost of heat little; what does change it is the length of the run (Section~\ref{sec:raceday}).

\subsection{Whether weather information improves prediction}

Both heat features lower held-out error (Figure~\ref{fig:mape_ladder}). Robust MAPE falls from 7.56\% for the weather-blind model to 7.50\% with the population curve, and to 7.33\% once the personalized score is added, a total of 0.23 points. On a 3:30 marathon the whole progression is worth about 29 seconds, and on a four-hour marathon about 33. This small magnitude is expected: the effect of heat is a small piece of daily training pace variation, where training intent, terrain and fatigue all have larger effects. Average error over a mostly temperate corpus is therefore a weak instrument for detecting it.

A negative control establishes that the personalized gain is runner-specific rather than an artifact of supplying the model one more numeric feature. The control reuses the personalized percentile with its distribution intact, reassigning each value to a different runner within the fold, so only the pairing between a runner and their own heat response is broken. If the gain came from the extra column alone, the shuffled version should reproduce it; instead it lands at 7.48\%, within noise of the population model. In runner-clustered bootstrap terms, the personalized gain beyond the population curve is 0.168 points, with a 95\% interval of [0.062, 0.283] excluding zero, while the shuffled interval [$-0.019$, 0.043] straddles it. The population curve's own gain of 0.060 points has an interval of [0.003, 0.118], which now excludes zero on the enlarged corpus. The current-inclusive, prior and strict-prior versions of the personalized score perform almost identically, so the gain does not depend on including the target activity.

\subsection{Limitations of the weather-blind model}

Stratifying held-out error by temperature exposes a systematic failure that average error conceals (Figure~\ref{fig:bias_fix}). The weather-blind model predicts runs too slow in cold and mild conditions, with a bias near $-2\%$, then crosses zero between 22 and $25\,^{\circ}$C ($+0.36\%$) and predicts too fast in the hottest band, at $+1.84\%$ for runs between 25 and $28\,^{\circ}$C. Its errors on hot days are therefore optimistic, and increasingly so as conditions worsen, which is the direction that matters for a runner planning a race.

Supplying the heat features removes that optimism. In the same hottest band, the population curve moves the bias to $-1.08\%$ and the personalized score to $-0.81\%$, so the sign flips from optimistic to conservative. For the 3:30 runner this is a shift from predicting 3.9 minutes too fast to 1.7 minutes too slow, and for the four-hour runner from 4.4 minutes too fast to 2.0 too slow. The personalized model holds the bias closest to zero in every band below $20\,^{\circ}$C and in the hottest band; between 20 and $25\,^{\circ}$C, where the weather-blind model is crossing zero, the blind model is closer (Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp}).

Two qualifications attach. The correction slightly overshoots at the hot end rather than landing on zero, trading an optimistic error for a conservative one about half its size. And the personalized model's bias curve runs nearly parallel to the population model's, which is the first indication that its contribution is a level shift rather than a temperature-specific one.

\subsection{What the personalized score measures}

The personalized score improves prediction and depends on being matched to the right runner, but three diagnostics indicate that it is not a measure of heat sensitivity alone.

Its effect is nearly independent of temperature. Relative to the population model it shifts predictions by about $-0.2$ to $-0.5\%$ in every temperature band, and the row-level correlation between that shift and WBGT is $+0.026$. The shift is instead ordered by the runner's score quintile, which is why the bias curves in Figure~\ref{fig:bias_fix} run parallel.

Forcing it to act through the temperature curve retains only part of its value. Supplied as a percentile-matched deviation of the curve, so that its input is zero at and below the $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, it yields 7.40\% robust MAPE, a gain of 0.098 points beyond the population curve with a 95\% interval of [0.041, 0.154]. That is a genuine temperature-shaped signal, but it is about 58\% of the 0.168-point gain the unconstrained score achieves. The remainder is runner-level information that is not specific to heat.

Calibrating both features against the correction the weather-blind model requires is best read as a sensitivity test rather than as a measurement, because the result depends on the specification. Fitted on the manuscript's baseline, the population curve's scale is $\alpha = 1.10$, with a 95\% interval of [0.91, 1.30] that includes one, so the magnitude the deep model learned agrees with the correction the pace model independently requires. The personalized deviation's coefficient is $\gamma = -0.96$ [$-1.71$, $-0.10$], negative: conditional on the population curve, runners the model marks as more heat-sensitive would require their predictions adjusted in the opposite direction.

Refitting that calibration while varying only how much runner-level information the baseline carries shows the coefficient is a property of the specification. With no runner-level features at all, $\gamma = -5.82$ [$-7.81$, $-3.88$]. With the manuscript's baseline, $-1.17$ [$-1.95$, $-0.39$] in this replication. Adding runner fixed effects to the calibration moves it to $+0.61$ [$-0.01$, $1.46$], and a per-runner intercept estimated from each runner's earlier activities gives $+0.15$ [$-0.27$, $0.73$]. Both intervals include zero. The population scale $\alpha$ moves along the same ladder in the same direction, from 3.31 with no runner-level information to 1.05 with the manuscript's baseline and 0.56 with the per-runner intercept, which is the population term absorbing the same runner-level confound before it is controlled.

The mechanism is a confound between runner identity and training climate. Across fitting runners, a runner's mean prediction error correlates with the wet-bulb globe temperature they habitually train in at $r = +0.19$, a slope of $+0.18$ percentage points per \textdegree C; the hottest habitual quartile carries a mean error of $+2.68$ points against $+0.80$ for the coolest. Any runner-level term therefore absorbs part of the heat signal without being shown a temperature, and a personalized heat feature estimated alongside one is left explaining a residual whose sign is not stable. Once runner identity is supplied directly, the personalized deviation adds nothing, which is the cleanest statement of what it was carrying.

Three things follow. The learned curve is the flattest of the corrections by a wide margin: its
residual bias sits between $-0.46$ and $-0.58\%$ at every threshold, a root-mean-square variation of
0.10 against 0.29 for the next best form, so almost none of what remains is predictable from
conditions. What remains is a small uniform over-correction, and the form whose residual comes nearest
zero in the hottest strata, Guy's capped slope, does so by crossing from under- to over-correction as
the threshold rises rather than by being flat. The learned curve is also the only form whose published magnitude needs essentially no rescaling
on training runs; fitting a free scale to it returns $1.09$, against 0.77--0.79 for Ely's and Guy's
elite forms and 1.46 for Mantzios's marathon slope. Forms estimated on mass fields over-correct severely when applied to training runs, by
factors of three to fourteen (the Gasparetto quadratics by more than an order of magnitude and are
omitted from the table), which is the expected direction given that race-day heat sensitivity exceeds
training-pace sensitivity, by the factor of about five measured in Section~\ref{sec:raceday}. And the elite forms, which are shallower, transfer well: Martin's quadratic
is the next flattest, though it achieves this partly by bending in cool conditions where the learned
curve is deliberately flat.

\subsection{Robustness to the choice of baseline}

Because that reversal is conditional on a baseline containing heart rate, the whole progression was repeated on a baseline that removes current effort entirely, using an independently trained marathon-fitness estimate with distance and elevation. This branch is supplementary and is not an equivalent comparison, since it changes the fitness representation and drops current effort at the same time, which raises its absolute error; the fixed fitness estimate is also available for only two thirds of the enlarged corpus, so the branch runs on 16{,}554 held-out activities from 103 runners. Within it, the population temperature information remains useful though its gain interval includes zero (0.09 points [$-0.10$, 0.25]), raw WBGT produces a gain of the same size, and the strict-prior personalization's gain over the population curve, 0.16 points [$-0.03$, 0.38], no longer excludes zero. The $\gamma$ reversal disappears ($\gamma = +1.12$ [$-0.92$, 3.31]), but the estimate is imprecise and unstable across runner folds. A narrower ablation that keeps the trailing-pace fitness proxy and removes only current heart rate gives the same picture with more precision: the population curve improves on the blind baseline by 0.03 points [0.01, 0.06], the personalized score by a further 0.09 [0.01, 0.18], and the shuffled control by 0.01 [$-0.01$, 0.03]. The result is that the personalized finding depends on the conditional baseline, and that this branch neither confirms nor refutes its magnitude.

\subsection{Acclimation}


The literature in Section~\ref{sec:literature} predicts that recent heat exposure should reduce a runner's heat penalty. Two tests examine whether the model's per-runner score behaves that way.

The first compares the score against each runner's recent training conditions. For every activity the number of runs above $18\,^{\circ}$C WBGT in the preceding fourteen days is counted, and the runner's prospective sensitivity rank is regressed on it within runner, with the current run's WBGT as a control and a placebo term for the fourteen days that follow. One standard deviation more recent hot training lowers the rank by 0.94 percentile points (runner-clustered 95\% interval [$-1.23$, $-0.64$]), which is the direction acclimation predicts, while the same count over the following fourteen days has no effect ($+0.08$ [$-0.19$, 0.34]), so the association is not a season or climate artifact. An exposure measure that decays with a fourteen-day half-life gives the same answer ($-0.73$ per standard deviation [$-1.13$, $-0.33$]). Across within-runner deciles of exposure the rank falls almost monotonically (every step after the second decile is downward), from $+0.9$ points in the least-exposed decile to $-1.6$ in the most exposed.

The second test uses an independently curated set of candidate acclimation episodes: pairs of hot runs, matched on conditions, recorded before and after a block of heat exposure, with a control set of pairs that bracket no such block. Across 465 low-to-high exposure episodes from 353 runners, performance in matched conditions improves after the block by 1.95\% [1.34, 2.56], and 63\% of episodes improve; across 450 low-to-low control episodes from 359 runners the improvement is 0.55\% [$-0.03$, 1.13]. The difference, 1.40 percentage points [0.54, 2.27], is the acclimation effect the design isolates.

The heart-rate channel is decisive where pace is not. At matched load, heat raises heart rate by 0.29\,bpm per \textdegree C above 15\,\textdegree C and by a further 0.39 above 22 (413{,}274 runs, 2{,}350 runners). That cost falls with recent exposure: each log-unit of hot runs in the preceding 21 days lowers it by 0.14\,bpm per \textdegree C ($p = 9\times10^{-15}$), and by 0.10 ($p = 5\times10^{-6}$) once training volume is controlled, which is the larger confound. Among measures with a month-long memory the flattening is specific to this count: the heat-exposure score with the production decay of 2.5\% a day and the hours run at or above 24\,\textdegree C over 28 days leave the slope unchanged, and a time-reversed placebo for those hours is as large as the count's effect. The natural experiments below, which follow exposure over days rather than months, show a clear heat-specific build and decay; a shift in heart rate at every WBGT that goes with recent heat fails a future-dose placebo and is not interpreted as acclimation. After 45 or more days without heat (2{,}468 episodes, 1{,}313 persons), the heart-rate excess on hot runs falls by 1.6\,bpm [1.0, 3.9] as hot-run days accumulate, half of it within 3.5 [1.4, 13.9] hot-run days (Figure~\ref{fig:kinetics}A). Runners arriving somewhere at least 5\,\textdegree C hotter than home (892 trips, 495 runners) start about 1.2\,bpm above the level of neutral trips, a figure that depends on the baseline used, and the excess declines by 0.23\,bpm per day over the first week [0.11, 0.36] (half-life 2.7 days [0.7, 10.5]), against $+0.06$ [$-0.07$, 0.19] on neutral and $+0.03$ [$-0.15$, 0.20] on cool trips (Figure~\ref{fig:kinetics}B). The count of days with a hot run describes the build better than calendar days (in 100\% of 500 person bootstraps) or ambient heat at the runner's location (98\%), and neither adds to it ($-0.5$ [$-1.4$, 0.3] and $+0.1$ [$-0.3$, 0.5]\,bpm). After a block of hot running (23{,}134 breaks, 1{,}134 persons) the first hot run back carries 0.5, 0.6, 1.5 and 2.1\,bpm more excess after 4--7, 8--14, 15--28 and 29--56 days off, a decay half-life of 9.7 days [2.1, 27.7] (Figure~\ref{fig:kinetics}C); the recursive state gives 11 days fitted to the onset episodes and 7 days fitted to every run. Up to about a third of the rise after a break also appears on non-hot runs (difference 1.4\,bpm [$-0.1$, 2.9] at 15--28 days), the level at saturation is not identified, and a shift in heart rate at every WBGT fails the future-dose placebo. By calendar month, hot runs by temperate northern-hemisphere runners carry a heart-rate excess of $+2.8$\,bpm in January and $-0.7$ in July (Figure~\ref{fig:onset}), a pattern that mixes acclimation with seasonal changes in fitness.

The race results show the pace-side counterpart at edition level. Adding the positive part of each edition's deviation from the 60-day pre-race climate at the start line to the race-day regression gives $+0.11\%$ of finish time per \textdegree C for marathons (95\% interval 0.04 to 0.18, $p = 0.002$, 321 editions) and $+0.11\%$ for half marathons (0.07 to 0.16, $p = 2\times10^{-6}$, 239 editions), and lowers the heat scale from 5.09 to 4.60 and from 4.21 to 3.51. On training runs the within-runner slope on the positive deviation from the trailing 60-day climate is $+0.22\%$ per \textdegree C.

The hometown design places the effect within person. Across 1{,}197{,}267 marathon finishes by 337{,}536 persons in 302 editions, the interaction of the curve with recent home climate is $-0.047\%$ of finish time per percent of curve per \textdegree C [$-0.069$, $-0.024$] ($p = 4\times10^{-5}$): a finisher whose home was warmer over the preceding 60 days loses less at a hot edition. Across 915{,}106 half-marathon finishes by 306{,}038 persons in 224 editions it is $-0.062$ [$-0.095$, $-0.029$] ($p = 2\times10^{-4}$). The term is $-0.075$ [$-0.156$, 0.007] in the 103 spring and $-0.078$ [$-0.119$, $-0.037$] in the 130 autumn marathon editions. Recent and annual home climate cannot be separated: entered together, their interactions take opposite signs, recent $-0.084$ and annual $+0.056$ for marathons but recent $+0.066$ and annual $-0.178$ for half marathons.

The two per-runner-score tests above are consistent with acclimation, and neither is decisive on its own. The episodes are candidates identified from training history rather than verified physiological measurements, and few runners contribute repeated episodes above $25\,^{\circ}$C (29 episodes from 24 runners), so no individual adaptation magnitude can be estimated. What the corpus supports is that acclimation is detectable as a population-level tendency in these data, and that the model's per-runner score moves with it in the expected direction. What the pace channel does not support is a per-runner acclimation state that could be tracked over a training block. The heart-rate channel shows onset on arrival in a hotter place and a lower heat cost after recent hot running, estimated from runs that consumer devices already record, but a run-by-run heart-rate acclimation state estimated before each race does not improve held-out prediction of race-distance efforts (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\subsection{Persistent differences between runners and race efforts}
\label{sec:perrunner}

At matched load, heat raises heart rate by 0.48\,bpm per \textdegree C above 15\,\textdegree C [0.45, 0.51] and by a further 0.43 [0.35, 0.51] above 24\,\textdegree C (368{,}039 runs, 2{,}341 persons), about 7\,bpm at WBGT 27 against 15. Runners differ around that cost (Figure~\ref{fig:between}A). The between-runner standard deviation of the slope is 0.34\,bpm per \textdegree C [0.31, 0.38] between alternate weeks, 0.28 [0.24, 0.34] between the early and late halves of a history (1{,}791 persons) and 0.29 [0.24, 0.34] between different heat seasons (1{,}304 persons): 66\% of the same-season variance persists, 74\% [62, 85] across summers, and a runner's full-history slope has a reliability near 0.61. Body-size, age, fitness and volume proxies together explain 4.7\% [0.2, 8.4] of the persistent variance, and the body-mass proxy alone 0.1\% [$-0.8$, 0.9]. Exposure measures with a month-long memory, the heat-exposure score with the production decay and the hours at or above 24\,\textdegree C over 28 days, do not change the slope ($+0.05$ [$-0.03$, 0.13] and $+0.01$ [$-0.05$, 0.07]\,bpm per \textdegree C between their tenth and ninetieth percentiles). The 21-day count of runs above 18\,\textdegree C does flatten it, by 0.14 [0.10, 0.18], and the same count over the following 21 days does not (0.00 [$-0.03$, 0.03]); the count accounts for 2 to 3\% of the persistent variance. A lower heart rate at every WBGT also goes with recent heat, but heat after the run predicts it as well, so it is not attributed to acclimation; the short-memory kinetics of Section~\ref{sec:results} carry the heat-specific effect.

Three groups is the resolution these estimates support (Figure~\ref{fig:between}B). Set on the first half of each runner's history, they separate the second half at 0.32, 0.51 and 0.62\,bpm per \textdegree C (gap 0.31 [0.24, 0.38], 1{,}789 runners), and the median runner's posterior probability of their own group is 56\% with three groups, 42\% with four and 33\% with five.

On race efforts the training heat slope does not carry over (Figure~\ref{fig:between}C). Among 1{,}308 hot efforts by 572 runners, the population model misses by 7.92 [7.48, 8.35] points of finish time on average; the training-derived trait changes that by $-0.02$ [$-0.06$, 0.04], the runner's earlier race efforts lower it by 0.52 [0.33, 0.72] to 7.41, and earlier efforts with the heat-exposure score by 0.63 [0.41, 0.85] to 7.30. Most of the gain is the runner's general miss against the predictor, which applies at every temperature: the heat-specific part alone gains 0.13 [0.07, 0.21]. Among runners with an earlier hot effort, thirds of the estimated heat trait lose 0.83, 1.03 and 1.17 times the typical heat cost (gap 0.34 [0.06, 0.59]), against 0.91, 0.98 and 1.22 (gap 0.31 [0.02, 0.60]) for thirds of the training-derived trait, and the median posterior probability of the most probable third is 0.38 after one earlier hot effort and 0.45 after five or more. 44\% of hot efforts have no earlier hot effort, and among them training-derived thirds differ by 0.11 [$-0.24$, 0.48]. Each unit of the heat-exposure score changes race-day heat cost by $-0.93$ [$-1.27$, $-0.62$] times the typical cost. Fixing the correlation between the two runner effects at zero leaves the held-out error unchanged ($+0.001$ [$-0.015$, 0.017]).

In official results, a runner's own race-day heat cost persists from race to race at most weakly (Figure~\ref{fig:between}D). With course-disjoint halves and only each year's weather at a course counting, the persistent between-runner variance of the marathon heat slope is $\tau^2 = 8.7$ [3.0, 14.3] in squared scale units (33{,}129 runners; 6.8 [1.0, 12.7] after the placebo is subtracted), a standard deviation near 2.6 scale units, about 3.5\% of finish time at WBGT 21\,\textdegree C. The estimate is fragile: its interval includes zero once personal trends are added or the halves also share no calendar year, and a random-slope model on all 562{,}388 repeat marathoners finds no persistent variance ($\tau^2$ below 0.3). A full race history measures a runner's slope with a median reliability near 0.04, so thirds assigned from it are right 39\% of the time against 33\% by chance; the half marathon does not identify $\tau^2$.

\subsection{The race-day scale of the curve}
\label{sec:raceday}

The linkage joins the 8{,}221{,}398 finishes into 3{,}927{,}315 persons, of whom 1{,}565{,}641 have two or more finishes, and yields 15{,}280{,}160 same-person pairs, 4{,}375{,}112 of them on the same course; edition effects are estimable on 92 courses (50 marathons and 42 half marathons). The member identifier disagrees with the linkage for 2.4\% of the persons on whom it can be checked (2{,}574 of 105{,}258), and for 1.9 to 2.9\% in the middle half of the 85 courses with at least 100 checks, which bounds the false-link rate. The outlier rule removes one edition, Capital City Half Marathon 2017 (median finish 1:41 against 2:09 to 2:14 in its other years). After the exclusions of Section~\ref{sec:raceday_methods}, 321 marathon editions of 49 races and 239 half-marathon editions of 40 races enter the regressions (5{,}852{,}530 finishes), with race-window WBGT from $-3.8$ to 23.4\,\textdegree C and from $-4.1$ to 25.9\,\textdegree C.

The within-person edition effects track the learned curve (Figure~\ref{fig:raceday}). With course and calendar-year fixed effects the scale is $\alpha = 5.09$ [4.52, 5.67] for the marathon (partial $R^2$ of the curve 0.54) and $\alpha = 4.21$ [3.62, 4.80] for the half marathon (0.52); in the pooled model with a band interaction the difference between the two is 1.07 [0.29, 1.85] ($p = 0.007$), and 1.03 [0.25, 1.81] ($p = 0.01$) once the degree-hour term introduced below is included. With a course-specific linear trend the scales are 5.16 [4.64, 5.69] and 4.18 [3.76, 4.60]. On the five-marathon subset for 2015--2025 the scale is 5.90 [4.23, 7.58] (44 editions), and 6.22 [4.75, 7.69] on 48 editions when the four London editions whose start time comes from a secondary source are added; with course effects only the 44-edition estimate is 6.45 [3.95, 8.94], and unweighted it is 5.74 [3.64, 7.85]. Within that subset, New York City 2022 (race-window WBGT 20.4\,\textdegree C) ran 5.7\% slower than its course and calendar year predict and Chicago 2021 (20.2\,\textdegree C) 5.1\% slower, against 5.5\% from the fitted scale and a training-run curve value of 1.05\% for both. The calendar-year effects carry the within-person trend, which repeat pairs cannot divide into the ageing of repeat finishers and changes in shoes or fields; a step at 2019 beyond a linear trend is $-0.8$ percentage points (standard error 0.6) for marathons and $-0.2$ (0.5) for half marathons.

The five marathons give a larger scale than all 49 for two reasons. On all 51 of their editions the five-marathon scale is 5.95 with their own calendar-year effects and 5.64 when the year effects are shared with the other courses; and in that pooled marathon model their scale exceeds the 4.52 of the other 44 races by 1.11 [0.02, 2.21] ($p = 0.046$). The all-marathon estimate is a precision-weighted average in which the five large fields carry 55\% of the weight; unweighted, or with equal total weight per course, it is 4.63 and 4.66. The gap does not come from field composition by finish time (median finish 4.46 against 4.38 hours), and it narrows slightly when the other races are restricted to the majors' range of conditions (4.66 at race-window WBGT up to 20.4\,\textdegree C). The scale does not rest on runners who slowed sharply: refitted on edition quantiles of runner deviations it is 5.21 [4.54, 5.87] at the median and 4.87 [4.27, 5.46] at the 25th percentile for the marathon, and 4.32 and 3.89 for the half. With finish-time tiers assigned from each runner's other finishes, the marathon scale is 4.7, 5.5, 5.7, 5.6, 5.1 and 4.0 from under 3:00 to over 5:00, highest between 3:30 and 4:30, and the half-marathon scale does not fall with finish time (3.2 to 4.8); tiers assigned from the same finishes that serve as outcomes produce a spurious fall with finish time by regression to the mean.

The estimates are sensitive to how race-day conditions are measured. Of the 561 editions in the regressions, the official information moved the race date for 76, the start line by more than 5\,km for 170 and the start time by more than 30 minutes for 248 (357 editions changed on at least one of the three); the mean absolute change in race-window WBGT is 1.3\,\textdegree C over all editions and 2.9, 2.2 and 1.8\,\textdegree C in those three groups, and 111 editions moved by more than 2\,\textdegree C. With the previous weather table restricted to the same editions the scale is 4.56 [3.89, 5.24] for the marathon and 4.23 [3.56, 4.89] for the half marathon, and on the five-marathon subset it is 5.23 [3.85, 6.61] on the same 48 editions, so the official inputs raise the marathon scale through the measurement of conditions and not through the sample, and leave the half-marathon scale unchanged. An earlier draft reported 4.9 [3.4, 6.5] for the five marathons on the previous weather windows; on the re-estimated edition effects those windows give 4.75 [3.49, 6.01], and the official windows, the identified first stage and the exclusion of the four secondary-source starts account for the rest of the change to 5.90.

The same scale appears inside the corpus, where each effort's weather is sampled along the run itself. On 2{,}011 race-labelled efforts by 817 runners (763 marathons and 1{,}248 half marathons), the curve's fitted scale is 4.89 [3.79, 5.98] within runner, 8.38 [5.73, 11.04] for the marathons and 4.04 [2.92, 5.17] for the half marathons, against 0.84 [0.60, 1.07] on the same runners' training runs. The training-run curve therefore needs a race-day scale of about five for the marathon and about four for the half from the official results, and the labelled efforts point the same way with wider intervals.

Part of the race-day scale is event length. Within runner on training runs, the curve's scale rises with expected duration: 0.21 [0.03, 0.38] under 30 minutes (87{,}099 runs), 0.81 [0.66, 0.95] at 30 to 60 minutes, 1.41 [1.24, 1.58] at 60 to 90, 1.71 [1.49, 1.94] at 90 to 150 and 2.34 [1.82, 2.87] over 150 minutes (7{,}046 runs by 997 runners), or 0.69 [0.56, 0.82] per log unit of duration (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}B); a placebo with one common scale of 0.79 is recovered at 0.74 to 0.81 in every band. Against the training scale of a run of matching length, the race-day scale is 2.46 [1.99, 2.93] times larger for the half marathon and 2.17 [1.63, 2.71] times for the marathon. Section~\ref{sec:gap} repeats the comparison within the corpus.

Official results of shorter races extend the gradient (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}B). The scale is 1.28 [1.19, 1.36] for the 5K (30{,}165 editions of 6{,}827 races, 10{,}936 at WBGT 18\,\textdegree C or above), 2.54 [2.26, 2.81] for the 10K (3{,}976 editions of 820 races, 1{,}126 warm) and 3.31 [2.70, 3.93] for 15\,km and 10 miles pooled (703 editions of 151 races, 165 warm; 2.99 without the race with the largest weight), with intervals clustered by course. Across ten distances from 5\,km to the marathon, $\alpha = -5.20 + 1.85\ln(\text{median finish time, min})$ (weighted $R^2$ 0.95), about 1.3 per doubling of race duration against 0.66 for training runs, so races cost 1.8 to 2.7 times the training-run scale at matched duration. In this source the marathon scale is 5.03 and the half-marathon scale 3.56; moving every start by one hour changes the scales by 3 to 12\%, and scheduled starts were within 15 minutes of official first mass starts in 97.5\% of 40 checked editions. Within each distance the scale is highest for mid-pack finishers and lower for the fastest and slowest.

Of the time-resolved terms, the rise in WBGT over the race window adds to the curve and the degree-hours are not identified. At matched mean WBGT, each degree of rise between the start and the median finisher's finish costs $+0.141\%$ of finish time in marathons [0.080, 0.203], $+0.177\%$ in half marathons [0.090, 0.263] and $+0.156\%$ pooled [0.107, 0.205]. Degree-hours of WBGT above 22\,\textdegree C, which are nonzero in 16 marathon editions and 8 half-marathon editions, carry $+0.159\%$ per \textdegree C\,h [$-0.062$, 0.379] next to the curve in marathons and $+0.340$ [$-0.530$, 1.210] in half marathons; under the earlier first stage the marathon coefficient was $-0.31$, so its sign depends on how the first stage is specified and no cumulative dose is estimated. The LASSO over ten race-day components with the curve unpenalised selects the rise in WBGT, relative humidity, dew point, gusts, cloud cover and the positive deviation in both bands (the rise in 100\% of bootstrap resamples for marathons and 92\% for half marathons), precipitation for marathons only, and mean wind speed and solar radiation for half marathons only; the spread of WBGT within the window is selected in neither. A physics-form wind-drag term fitted alongside the curve on all courses, with course bearings taken as uniformly spread, gives $0.07$ [$-0.23$, 0.37] for marathons, $-0.14$ [$-0.49$, 0.21] for half marathons and $0.00$ [$-0.22$, 0.23] pooled, against a value near 2 that unsheltered drag physics would imply; without the point-to-point courses it is $-0.13$ [$-0.38$, 0.13] and $0.10$ [$-0.30$, 0.50]. On the corpus's training runs the drag coefficient is measurable but small, 0.25 [0.12, 0.38], about one eighth of the unsheltered value (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

Official splits locate the heat cost within the race. Across 368{,}545 finishers in 50 editions of nine races (race-window WBGT up to 22.3\,\textdegree C), the change in second-half minus first-half pace between editions of a course is predicted worst by a uniform rule (root-mean-square error 3.29 percentage points over 20 warm edition comparisons) and by a rule in which each point of the race pays for the WBGT at that point (2.46; New York City 2022, a day of flat WBGT, slowed by 3.76 points more in the second half against 0.27 predicted). A fade-shaped rule (1.56), heat multiplied by position in the race (1.59) and a storage bank (1.79) fit about equally against a noise floor near 0.7 and cannot be told apart. Over the 15 warm editions, 0.79 [0.47, 1.11] of the edition's heat cost appears as extra second-half slowing.

\subsection{Heart rate, cadence and stride length in heat}
\label{sec:mechanics}

Among 363{,}005 runs with cadence by 2{,}325 runners (3{,}521 at WBGT 28\,\textdegree C or above), pace relative to 8 to 10\,\textdegree C is 1.01 [0.80, 1.23]\% slower at 20 to 22\,\textdegree C, 2.34 [1.82, 2.87]\% at 24 to 26\,\textdegree C and 4.17 [3.51, 4.82]\% at 28\,\textdegree C or above; at matched heart rate the differences are 1.69 [1.49, 1.90], 3.79 [3.21, 4.38] and 6.60 [5.90, 7.30]\% (Figure~\ref{fig:channels}). At matched pace, heart rate is higher by 1.87 [1.63, 2.12], 4.12 [3.61, 4.62] and 7.08 [6.42, 7.74]\,bpm, and cadence changes by $-0.49$ [$-0.58$, $-0.40$], $-0.70$ [$-0.82$, $-0.58$] and $-0.98$ [$-1.22$, $-0.75$]\%. At the same speed relative to the runner's 30-day pace, heart rate at WBGT 24\,\textdegree C or above exceeds heart rate below 10\,\textdegree C by 1.27 to 2.32\,bpm across speed bins and cadence differs by $-1.65$ to $-0.84$\% (Figure~\ref{fig:hrcadspeed}); with speed relative to the runner's whole-record mean pace the ranges are 1.42 to 3.04\,bpm and $-1.29$ to $-0.54$\% (correlation of the two relative speeds 0.83; Figure~\ref{fig:fixedbaseline}).

The speed difference at 20 to 22\,\textdegree C ($-1.01$ [$-1.23$, $-0.80$]\%) comprises $-0.73$ [$-0.83$, $-0.63$] percentage points of cadence and $-0.28$ [$-0.48$, $-0.09$] of stride length (72\% cadence); at 28\,\textdegree C or above the speed difference is $-4.17$ [$-4.82$, $-3.51$]\%, with $-1.97$ [$-2.27$, $-1.68$] from cadence and $-2.19$ [$-2.73$, $-1.66$] from stride length (47\% cadence; Figure~\ref{fig:speedsplit}). Within runs (57{,}580 runs, 1{,}783 runners), from the first half to the second at 28\,\textdegree C or above, relative to 10 to 15\,\textdegree C, heart rate rises by a further 2.24 [1.72, 2.75]\,bpm, speed changes by $-2.44$ [$-2.96$, $-1.93$]\% and cadence by $-1.06$ [$-1.27$, $-0.86$]\% (Figure~\ref{fig:withinrun}).

On cool training runs (136{,}000 runs, 2{,}039 runners), cadence carries 24 [23, 25]\% of a within-runner speed difference (24 [23, 24]\% with 30-day pace in the model): 36 [34, 38]\% in the slowest third of a runner's own speeds and 20 [18, 22]\% and 21 [20, 22]\% in the middle and fastest thirds. At that share, the speed difference at 20 to 22\,\textdegree C ($-1.01$\%) corresponds to a cadence difference of $-0.24$\%, against $-0.73$\% observed, and the difference at 28\,\textdegree C or above ($-4.17$\%) to $-1.00$\%, against $-1.97$\% observed. Cadence carries 19 [18, 19]\% of the change in speed between the halves of a run below 15\,\textdegree C and 20 [19, 22]\% at 24\,\textdegree C or above.

In 57{,}492 runs with both halves recorded (1{,}783 runners), heart rate rises from the first half to the second by 2.9 [2.7, 3.0]\,bpm below 10\,\textdegree C, 3.2 [3.0, 3.4]\,bpm at 10 to 15\,\textdegree C, 4.4 [4.2, 4.5]\,bpm at 20 to 24\,\textdegree C, 5.2 [5.0, 5.5]\,bpm at 24 to 28\,\textdegree C and 5.8 [5.1, 6.4]\,bpm at 28\,\textdegree C or above (1.9 [1.8, 2.0]\% and 3.8 [3.4, 4.2]\% of first-half heart rate in the coolest and hottest bands; 5.7 [5.4, 6.0] and 11.7 [10.4, 13.1]\,bpm per hour; Figure~\ref{fig:benchdrift}A). Drift is 0.55 [0.54, 0.57]\,bpm lower per 1\% of second-half slowing; at an unchanged speed it is 2.9, 3.4, 4.8, 5.8 and 7.1\,bpm in the five bands, and in steady runs of 45 to 90 minutes it is 2.8 [2.6, 3.1]\,bpm below 10\,\textdegree C (3{,}519 runs) and 7.4 [6.4, 8.3]\,bpm at 28\,\textdegree C or above (147 runs). Decoupling averages 2.0 [1.9, 2.2]\% below 10\,\textdegree C and 6.2 [5.6, 6.7]\% at 28\,\textdegree C or above, and the share of runs with decoupling above 5\% rises from 26\% to 58\% (Figure~\ref{fig:benchdrift}B, C); within runner, relative to 10 to 15\,\textdegree C, decoupling differs by $-0.5$ [$-0.6$, $-0.3$] percentage points below 10\,\textdegree C and by 1.3 [1.1, 1.4], 2.4 [2.2, 2.6] and 3.8 [3.4, 4.2] points in the three warmer bands. Per degree of WBGT above 15\,\textdegree C at matched pace, heart rate rises by 0.41 [0.37, 0.45]\,bpm (0.28 [0.26, 0.31]\%) and cadence changes by $-0.043$ [$-0.052$, $-0.034$]\%. With the change in speed between halves held fixed, the within-run cadence change at 28\,\textdegree C or above relative to 10 to 15\,\textdegree C is $-0.59$ [$-0.74$, $-0.43$]\%, against $-1.06$ [$-1.27$, $-0.86$]\% without the adjustment (55\% of it remaining; $-0.57$ [$-0.72$, $-0.41$]\% with a quadratic term and $-0.56$ [$-0.72$, $-0.41$]\% with a separate slope in heat), and the heart-rate change is 3.62 [3.21, 4.04]\,bpm, against 2.24 [1.72, 2.75]\,bpm.

By band of the run's mean heart rate, the pace difference at 28\,\textdegree C or above is 4.14 [3.24, 5.05]\% below 75\% of maximal heart rate (102{,}182 runs), 5.39 [4.66, 6.12]\% at 75 to 85\% (201{,}062) and 9.06 [8.03, 10.09]\% at 85\% or more (59{,}761; Figure~\ref{fig:effortbands}). With heart rate adjusted for the average heat difference before banding, the three values are 1.69 [0.94, 2.45], 3.04 [2.22, 3.86] and 7.20 [5.50, 8.91]\% (5.9\% of runs change band), and in thirds of each runner's own heart rate at the same WBGT they are 3.00 [1.95, 4.05], 4.04 [3.13, 4.94] and 7.39 [6.19, 8.59]\% (Figure~\ref{fig:banddefs}). All three definitions use heart rate recorded during the run, which heat itself raises.

On race-distance efforts (3{,}025 efforts, 703 runners), each percentage point of typical heat cost changes speed by $-1.14$ [$-1.28$, $-0.99$]\%, cadence by $-0.34$ [$-0.40$, $-0.28$]\% and stride length by $-0.80$ [$-0.90$, $-0.69$]\% (cadence share 30 [26, 34]\%), and changes the within-effort cadence change by $-0.12$ [$-0.16$, $-0.07$]\%. With the effort's cadence in the model the heat slope of the loss is 0.48 [0.35, 0.59] of its unconditional value, so that 52 [44, 62]\% of the slope moves with cadence. At WBGT 18\,\textdegree C or above, the heat cost is 0.97 [0.84, 1.09] times the typical cost over all 1{,}278 efforts with cadence. It is 0.82 [0.68, 0.98] where cadence holds within the effort (800 efforts) and 1.20 [1.04, 1.35] where it falls (478); 0.91 [0.67, 1.13] and 1.61 [1.41, 1.82] against the runner's own cool efforts (296 and 399); and 0.93 [0.78, 1.08] and 1.07 [0.91, 1.25] against the runner's training cadence at the same speed (897 and 381; Figure~\ref{fig:racecadence}).

On race-distance efforts with halves split by distance (4{,}957 efforts, 1{,}217 runners), second-half slowing relative to 10 to 15\,\textdegree C is greater by 1.1 [0.3, 1.9], 1.9 [1.1, 2.8], 3.4 [2.1, 4.7] and 8.2 [5.2, 11.2] percentage points at 15 to 18, 18 to 22, 22 to 26 and 26\,\textdegree C or above (92 efforts by 65 runners in the last band), and decoupling by 0.8 [0.1, 1.4], 2.0 [1.3, 2.7], 4.0 [2.9, 5.1] and 8.0 [5.3, 10.7] points, whereas heart-rate drift differs by $-0.4$ [$-0.8$, 0.0], 0.0 [$-0.5$, 0.5], 0.6 [$-0.1$, 1.3] and $-0.2$ [$-1.5$, 1.1] points (Figure~\ref{fig:racebands}). Unadjusted, heart-rate drift is 3.2 [3.0, 3.5]\% below 10\,\textdegree C and 3.5 [2.5, 4.6]\% at 26\,\textdegree C or above, and decoupling exceeds 5\% in 42\% and 74\% of efforts. The cadence change differs by $-0.3$ [$-0.5$, $-0.1$], $-0.5$ [$-0.8$, $-0.3$], $-0.8$ [$-1.2$, $-0.5$] and $-2.2$ [$-3.0$, $-1.3$] points across the four bands; cadence changes by $-0.149$ [$-0.165$, $-0.133$]\% per 1\% of second-half slowing, and at equal slowing the differences are $-0.13$ [$-0.30$, 0.05], $-0.24$ [$-0.44$, $-0.04$], $-0.35$ [$-0.66$, $-0.04$] and $-0.92$ [$-1.69$, $-0.16$] points. Cadence carries 16 [14, 18]\% of the change in speed between halves on efforts below 15\,\textdegree C and 15 [12, 18]\% at 18\,\textdegree C or above.

\subsection{Training and race-day cost within the corpus}
\label{sec:gap}

Fitted to all training runs, the scale on the curve is 0.79 [0.65, 0.94], which is 4.30 below the official marathon scale and 3.42 below the official half-marathon scale. The same runners' race-distance efforts, analyzed as official results are (finish time within runner and distance, a broader set of efforts than the race-labelled ones of Section~\ref{sec:raceday}), give 5.64 [3.36, 7.92] for the marathon (735 efforts, 252 runners) and 4.50 [3.88, 5.12] for the half marathon (3{,}567, 796), which differ from the official scales by 0.55 [$-1.80$, 2.90] and 0.29 [$-0.57$, 1.14] (Figure~\ref{fig:gap}). \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Implications}

Literature on heat's effect on endurance running and prediction modeling have developed separately. Research quantifying heat effects is retrospective: it regresses observed finishing times on the conditions those races happened to be run in on a field-wide level. Predictive running models, including those shipped with consumer running platforms, work from training history and take no account of race-day conditions. This paper joins the two: it predicts pace for a run that has not yet taken place based on that runner's prior training and forecasted conditions. In doing so, it becomes the first prospective, individual-level pace prediction to incorporate a heat response.

The population curve that results is well scaled rather than merely correlated with conditions. On training runs its calibration coefficient is compatible with one, meaning the magnitude the deep model learned matches the correction the pace model independently requires; on official race results the same shape applies at 5.1 times that scale for the marathon and 4.2 times for the half marathon, which places it inside the band of published mass-field forms once the training-to-race factor is accounted for. Its most consequential property is directional. A weather-blind model predicts runs in the hottest conditions faster than they were actually run, and supplying the curve removes that optimism. Because the error such a model makes is systematically in the runner's disfavor on precisely the days when conditions matter most, correcting its direction is worth more than the small reduction in average error suggests. The magnitude is quantifiable. At training-run scale the curve implies roughly nine additional minutes at $28\,^{\circ}$C for a runner whose ideal-weather marathon prediction is 3:30 and ten for a four-hour runner; at the race-day scale of Section~\ref{sec:raceday} a marathon at 21\,\textdegree C WBGT costs about 6.9\% of finish time, or fifteen minutes for the 3:30 runner, and 28\,\textdegree C would cost over 20\%, though no marathon edition in the validation set reached that far (the hottest had a race-window WBGT of 23.4\,\textdegree C).

The device records are consistent with a race-day scale that reflects how races are run more than a different physiological multiplier. At matched pace, heart rate is higher and cadence lower in heat, and stride length shortens most in the hottest conditions (Figures~\ref{fig:channels} and \ref{fig:speedsplit}). The same runners' race-distance efforts carry a scale of 5.64 [3.36, 7.92] for the marathon and 4.50 [3.88, 5.12] for the half marathon, close to the official scales, so most of the difference between training and race-day cost lies between a runner's long training runs and that runner's race-distance efforts, with a smaller part between short and long training runs and little of it between the two cohorts (Figure~\ref{fig:gap}).

Cadence falls in heat by two to three times what the cool-day relation between cadence and speed implies, and about half of the fall within a run remains when the change in speed is held fixed, so the lower step rate is not only a consequence of running more slowly. Field studies agree that late-race slowing comes with both shorter strides and a lower step rate \citep{hanley2020,hirakawa2026,winter2017,giovanelli2017}, and in ordinary running step rate changes little with speed: across treadmill speeds it rose by 4\% while stride length rose by 28\% \citep{cavanagh1989}, and in device records of recreational runners it rose by about 3 strides per minute per m/s \citep{vanoeveren2019}. The cool-day share of about a quarter found here is somewhat above the treadmill figure, and the share in heat is several times it. Studies of heat and running gait have been small and short: they found no change specific to heat at a fixed submaximal speed \citep{mtibaa2019,girard2017} or over a road race or triathlon \citep{dejonglempke2025,james2025}, and a 2.6\% lower step frequency in heat only in repeated sprints \citep{girard2017}; we found no study that relates the within-run change in cadence to ambient heat in a large sample of recorded runs. Late in recreational marathons heart rate rises relative to speed \citep{smyth2022,billat2020}; in training runs here, heart-rate drift and decoupling also rise with WBGT at an unchanged speed (Figure~\ref{fig:benchdrift}). The added drift is far smaller than in laboratory trials at a fixed power output, in which heart rate rose by 11\% against 2\% between 15 and 45 minutes at 35 and 22\,\textdegree C \citep{lafrenz2008} and by 12 to 19\% at 35\,\textdegree C in cycling and running \citep{wingo2005,wingo2020}; outdoors, speed is free to fall, and when pace is free, power output falls early in the heat \citep{tucker2004}. On race-distance efforts, by contrast, heart-rate drift does not differ by WBGT while slowing and decoupling do (Figure~\ref{fig:racebands}): near the highest sustainable output, added strain is paid in speed rather than in heart rate, as in laboratory trials in which heart rate at the lactate and ventilatory thresholds was unchanged at 35 against 18\,\textdegree C while power fell by about 10 to 17\% \citep{maunder2021}, and power at a matched heart rate was 17\% lower at 33\,\textdegree C \citep{charoensap2023}. The larger race-day cost of efforts whose cadence falls is not specific to heat, since such efforts also lose more on cool days and the two groups' multiples differ little net of that (Figure~\ref{fig:cadencechecks}). This agrees with a marathon study in which the greater deterioration of runners whose heart rate rose most relative to speed disappeared after adjustment for speed \citep{hunter2026b}, and with ultramarathon runners whose step rate did not change independently of speed \citep{burns2019}; a within-run cadence fall of about 1\% is also within the range in which stride frequency stays at its metabolic optimum during fatiguing runs \citep{hunter2007,vernillo2019}. Cadence decay therefore marks a fading effort more than it identifies a separate heat mechanism.

The per-runner result is more equivocal. A score matched to the correct runner improves prediction reproducibly, and shuffling that match removes the gain, so the score carries genuine individual information. Part of its effect is temperature-shaped: forced to act only through the temperature curve, as a percentile-matched deviation that is zero at and below the $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, it retains 58\% of the unconstrained gain, so a runner scored as more sensitive does require a progressively slower prediction as conditions worsen; the remainder is a runner-level shift that does not vary with temperature. What does not survive is conditioning: the residual direction inverts once heart rate enters the baseline. A linear replication of the calibration makes the dependence on the conditioning set explicit: with heart rate in the baseline the personalized deviation's coefficient is $+0.24$ per unit [$-0.69$, 1.17] under linear controls and $-0.95$ [$-1.89$, 0.01] under spline controls, neither distinguishable from zero, whereas with current heart rate removed it is $+0.87$ [0.16, 1.58] under linear controls and $-0.21$ [$-0.93$, 0.53] under spline controls; the sign is not stable across estimators once heart rate is removed either, so the reversal is a property of the conditioning set and the estimator together rather than of the score alone. Both results are what would be expected if heat sensitivity were already partly expressed through the physiological signals the baseline observes, in which case the personalized feature absorbs what remains rather than the effect itself. Replacing the population curve by each runner's own curve, without a separate rank, is consistent with that reading: error is essentially unchanged (7.51\% for the substituted curve against 7.50\% for the population curve alone, and 7.56\% with no heat term at all) and the bias moves in the physiologically expected direction ($-1.30\%$ against $-1.64\%$), but the gain that the separate rank supplies (7.33\%) does not appear, which is the signature of runner-level rather than heat-specific information. Separating a runner's heat sensitivity from its own downstream consequences therefore requires either a baseline that excludes those consequences or measurements that identify them directly, and the sensitivity analysis reported above shows that the first is not sufficient on its own.

\subsection{Who is in this corpus, and which way that biases the estimate}
\label{sec:selection}

The corpus is not a sample of runners, and it is not a sample of races. It is a sample of training runs
recorded by people who chose to wear a GPS watch and to share the data with a fitness platform. Three
selection mechanisms follow, and each has a predictable direction, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:selection}.

Runs are overwhelmingly training rather than racing. A runner who is not competing can slow down,
shorten, reschedule to a cooler hour, or move indoors when conditions are bad, and all four responses
either remove the observation or blunt it. The effect estimated here is therefore the effect on a runner
free to concede, which is a lower bound on the effect at a race, where the runner is not.

The people are self-selected toward commitment. Opting into a training platform, wearing a monitor
consistently, and accumulating the thirty-activity history the model requires all select for volume and
consistency, and plausibly for fitness and heat acclimatization. More acclimatized runners are less
heat-sensitive, so this too biases the population curve downward.

And within a runner, hot days are not randomly assigned to sessions. Hot runs in this corpus are
slightly shorter (within runner, about 0.2\,km less per 10\,\textdegree C of WBGT), though not measurably easier by relative heart rate. Since the training-run penalty rises with the length of the run
(Section~\ref{sec:raceday}), conditioning on the runs that actually happened understates what heat would
have cost had the same session been attempted regardless.

The mechanisms point the same way, which is convenient for interpretation if uncomfortable for
precision: the population curve should be read as a floor on race-day cost rather than an unbiased
estimate of it. Section~\ref{sec:raceday} puts a number on that floor: race-day finish times follow the
same curve at about five times the scale, which is consistent with Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}, where forms
fitted on actual mass-participation races imply substantially steeper slopes than the one learned here. It is also why no
attempt is made to extrapolate to championship conditions: this corpus cannot speak to a runner who has
no option to concede.

\subsection{Limitations}

The primary caveat is that this is a runner-disjoint held-out prediction, not a formal causal estimate of heat's physiological effect. The corpus is training runs, where improvements are calibration and accuracy gains on held-out training run pace rather than measured heat responses. The official results of Section~\ref{sec:raceday} supply the race-day scale, but at the level of whole editions rather than of individual runners: they show that the population curve is the right shape for race day and how much steeper it is there, not whether an individual's sensitivity transfers. Per-runner race-day tests are now possible (Section~\ref{sec:perrunner}). On the corpus's own race-distance efforts a runner's training heat slope does not improve prediction, and earlier races do so mostly by correcting the runner's general miss against the predictor; in official results, a runner's own heat cost persists from race to race at most weakly and a full race history measures it with a reliability near 0.04. What these tests leave open is whether a heat tolerance specific to the person exists at a size a forecast could use; linking the corpus runners to their official results would test it directly. The headline amplitude also moved during training: the median single-window penalty at $28\,^{\circ}$C, before the window averaging that gives the reported 4.3\%, was 4.8\% at one million steps and 4.2\% at the reported checkpoint, a change of about twelve percent while the shape of the curve held, so the reported magnitude carries a training-length uncertainty of that order on top of its sampling interval.

Second, runs at the extreme hot end are sparse. The held-out cohort is bounded above at $28\,^{\circ}$C by the ceiling imposed in Section~\ref{sec:methods} based on sparsity; the enlarged corpus more than doubled the runs above that point, which is what made the ceiling supportable at 28 rather than 25\,\textdegree C. Even below it, the warm bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones (1{,}625 held-out activities between 25 and $28\,^{\circ}$C against 5{,}398 between 10 and 15), leading to the hot-end estimates carrying the most sampling uncertainty. Denser sampling of genuinely hot runs would tighten these estimates.

Third, the per-runner result is narrower than it first appears. The score carries genuine individual information, since matching it to the correct runner improves held-out prediction and scrambling the assignment removes the gain. But the diagnostics in Section~\ref{sec:results} show that information is not heat sensitivity specifically: the effect is roughly constant across temperature, only about 58\% survives when constrained to act through the temperature curve, and the direct calibration returns the wrong sign. Either the baseline's heart rate has already slowed heat-sensitive runners on the same evidence, leaving the score to push back against a correction already made, or the deep model has the ordering wrong. Removing heart rate eliminates the reversal, favoring the first explanation, but too imprecisely to settle it. Until a baseline exists in which it is clear what each feature stands in for, the score is usable as evidence that a runner tends to be over- or under-predicted, not as a measurement of how much heat costs them.

Fourth, the mechanical and within-corpus results of Sections~\ref{sec:mechanics} and \ref{sec:gap} rest on device summaries and on unconfirmed races. Cadence is the device's mean over the run and stride length is derived from speed and cadence, not measured; watch cadence agrees closely with motion capture \citep{adams2016} and, unlike stride length, with foot-mounted sensors \citep{meingast2025}. Uphill gradient lowers cadence in watch data \citep{chan2025}, and climb per kilometer controls for it only on average over the run. Percentage heart-rate drift uses a first-half heart rate reconstructed from the whole-run mean, and only 92 race-distance efforts by 65 runners reach a WBGT of 26\,\textdegree C or above. Bands of heart rate recorded during the run condition on a response to heat, so the gradient across them is not an effect of effort. The race-distance efforts are not confirmed races, the marathon scale estimated from them has a wide interval, and the match to official editions uses date, distance and start line only. On those efforts the heat-exposure score is correlated with the typical cost, so a proportional effect cannot be separated from a level effect, and part of its association with the loss runs through the fitness prediction.

Finally, some of what the literature flags as relevant is simply absent from the data. Raw GPX files carry no body mass, though heat production scales with it and smaller runners are advantaged in heat \citep{marino2008, maughan2010}; no biological sex, an established performance differential under heat \citep{llewellyn2025, vihma2010, kelly2023, weiss2022b}; and no resting heart rate, one of the most validated acclimation markers \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, pryor2019}. Each would plausibly improve prediction. Laboratory variables such as plasma volume or sweat sodium concentration are further out of reach, since they cannot be obtained from consumer devices at all.

\subsection{Future extensions}
\label{sec:future}

Several extensions follow from the results above; each is stated with the evidence that motivates it and the form it should take, so that they can be built without relitigating the design.

\emph{A physics-form wind term with a calibrated coefficient.} Aerodynamic drag scales with the square of air speed, so the extra cost of wind on a course segment with along-path headwind $H$ is proportional to $(v+H)^2 - v^2 = 2vH + H^2$, averaged over the runner's route and race hours; on a loop the linear term cancels while the quadratic does not, and on a point-to-point course the linear term dominates and changes sign with the wind. Estimated on 330{,}000 corpus runs with 30-second GPS bearings, within runner and after the heat curve, the coefficient on this term is 0.25 [0.12, 0.38] in percent of pace per unit of relative drag work, against a value near 2 that unsheltered physics would imply for a runner at three metres per second; the edition-level estimate on all courses is 0.00 [$-0.22$, 0.23]. Mass fields shelter and draft, and reanalysis wind is a coarse measure of what a runner meets at street level, so the empirical coefficient is the one to use. At that coefficient a strong headwind on a point-to-point course costs about one percent and wind on a loop course a fraction of that, so the term belongs in a calculator as the one-parameter formula above rather than as a learned model: an earlier attempt to give the transformer a headwind channel degraded its fitness reconstruction, and there is under one percent of pace to recover.

\emph{Course terms from same-person official results.} Same-person pairs across courses identify course effects. On 1{,}179{,}868 pairs of marathon finishes on two of 49 courses no more than two years apart, net of each edition's heat and of the calendar year, New York City costs $+2.65\%$ relative to Chicago (standard error 0.04), London $+1.47\%$ (0.07), Boston $+1.27\%$ (0.04) and Big Sur $+5.77\%$ (0.10), close to the earlier five-course estimates ($+2.5$, $+2.1$, $+1.2$ and $+5.4$), whereas a grade-adjusted cost of each course's measured elevation profile gives $+0.6$, $-0.0$, $-0.2$ and $+1.5$ percentage points. The remainder is what terrain does not carry: turns, bridges, crowding, how a course is raced and who chooses to race it (Boston requires a qualifying time, and with all pairs regardless of the years between them its effect is $-0.15\%$). Across the 49 marathon courses the effects run from $-1.9$ to $+3.2\%$ between the tenth and ninetieth percentiles. A hierarchical course model with the terrain term as its prior mean, fitted to those pairs, is the natural next component of a race-day predictor.

\emph{Distance-specific race scales.} After the heat curve is applied, the within-runner residual is $-0.6\%$ on hot runs under 8\,km and $+1.3$ to $+1.4\%$ on hot runs of 18\,km and longer, so a single curve averaged over mostly short training runs over-corrects short efforts and under-corrects long ones. The race scales of Section~\ref{sec:raceday} are two points on the same gradient, now estimated from official results: 4.21 [3.62, 4.80] for the half marathon and 5.09 [4.52, 5.67] for the marathon, a difference that excludes zero. Official results of shorter races now cover the range below the half marathon: across ten distances the scale follows $\alpha = -5.20 + 1.85\ln(\text{median finish time, min})$, from 1.3 for the 5K to about 5 for the marathon (Section~\ref{sec:raceday}). A predictor should carry the scale as this function of race duration rather than as one number; what remains open is the response above about 26\,\textdegree C WBGT, where every distance thins out and none has data above 28\,\textdegree C.

\emph{Time-resolved conditions.} The rise in WBGT during the race window is supported at edition level ($+0.12\%$ of finish time per \textdegree C of rise at matched mean, pooled over both distances; Section~\ref{sec:raceday}) and is significant within runner on the corpus ($+0.2\%$ per standard deviation), as is solar radiation beyond what WBGT carries. A cumulative dose is not identified at race level: the degree-hour coefficient beside the curve changes sign with the first-stage specification on the 16 marathon and 8 half-marathon editions where it is nonzero, and in the deep model a gate given the dose lost to a plain heat gate (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). The supported form is therefore the curve evaluated on the conditions a runner meets over their own hours on the course rather than at the gun, which a forecast permits directly, with the cost distributed toward the second half of the race as the official splits show; whether a dose term appears in hotter races than these results contain remains open.

\emph{Acclimation as a tracked state.} The heart-rate results of Section~\ref{sec:results} supply the kinetics a tracker needs: half of the build comes within about three days with a hot run and the adaptation fades with a half-life near ten days, both counted in days with a hot run rather than in calendar days or ambient heat. A score with these rates (gain 0.15 per day of full stimulus, decay about 0.08 per day, heat counted from 22\,\textdegree C WBGT) fits held-out heart rate 1.7 to 1.9 times better than the literature setting of 2.5\% a day; whether race-day pace loss follows the same kinetics is not yet tested. A run-by-run heart-rate state with a 56-day window does not improve prediction of race efforts, whereas the exposure score counted from 22\,\textdegree C does (Section~\ref{sec:perrunner}). On the pace side, each degree by which conditions exceed the trailing 60-day climate costs about 0.2\% within runner on training runs, and 0.11\% for both distances at edition level on race results; the term should not be stacked on the population curve, which already reflects runners at their usual acclimation, and its use is the climate-move case, where a runner races somewhere hotter than they trained. The hometown-climate test shows that the deviation acts per person: a warmer recent home climate lowers the heat cost in marathons and in half marathons, in spring and in autumn editions alike, and carrying it into a per-runner prediction remains to be done.

\emph{Per-runner tolerance.} The per-runner score from the transformer is not separable from fitness (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). A runner's heart-rate response to heat at matched load has a persistent component (between-runner standard deviation 0.28\,bpm per \textdegree C against a population slope of 0.48, full-history reliability near 0.61) that is not confounded with fitness, but its precision supports at most three groups, and on held-out race efforts it does not improve prediction (Section~\ref{sec:perrunner}). Earlier race efforts do improve it, mostly by correcting the runner's general miss against the race predictor; the heat-specific part they carry is small, and groups formed from it are seldom confident (median posterior probability of the most probable third 0.38 after one earlier hot effort, 0.45 after five or more). In official results, a runner's own heat cost persists from race to race at most weakly and a full race history measures it with a reliability near 0.04. A deployed prediction should therefore carry the population curve, the runner's acclimation state and the runner's general miss, and treat any heat-tolerance group as a weak, evidence-dependent adjustment that defaults to typical.

\emph{Calibration of the training-run curve under a physiological baseline.} In the paper's own harness with heart rate in the baseline, the curve over-corrects within runner by about a quarter (residual slope on the curve $-0.23 \pm 0.05$), and a small cold cost of about $+0.3\%$ remains below 4\,\textdegree C where the curve is flat. Both are second-order relative to the race-day scale but should be folded into any deployed calibration.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Heat slows recreational distance runners little below a WBGT of about 14\,\textdegree C and progressively more above it. A transformer trained on runners' own training streams learns that shape without a parametric form, and the same curve describes race-day finish times in official results at a scale that rises with race duration, from about 1.3 for the 5K to about 5 for the marathon, roughly twice the cost of training runs of matched duration. Heart rate at matched load shows heat acclimation building within a few hot runs and fading with a half-life near ten days. Runners differ persistently in their heart-rate response to heat in training, but neither that trait nor a runner's own race history predicts an individual's race-day heat cost with useful reliability.

What is new relative to the literature is the combination of sources and the questions it makes answerable. The heat curve is learned from individuals' free-living training records and tested, through its scale, against independent official results; the ratio of race-day to training-run cost is measured across ten distances rather than assumed; within-person race-day estimates use weather over each edition's sourced race window; acclimation kinetics come from natural experiments in routine wearable data rather than laboratory protocols; and the transfer of individual heat sensitivity from training to race day is tested directly, with a largely negative answer that bounds how far a heat adjustment can be personalised. For a forward-looking predictor, the population curve at a duration-dependent race-day scale is the supported correction.
