{\large\textbf{Abstract}}\\[1pt]
\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}

\begin{singlespace}\small

\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Motivation.}} Anthropogenic warming is making extreme-heat events more frequent, and large road races increasingly take place in conditions their fields did not train for. Across the six Abbott World Marathon Majors, abnormally hot editions remain rare but have arrived roughly six times more often per year since 2020 than in the preceding four decades. Finish-time prediction has improved over the same period, drawing on the training data now collected passively by GPS watches, but the predictors in current use remain weather-blind, and are therefore systematically optimistic on precisely the days when a runner most needs a dependable estimate.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Literature.}} Heat slows distance runners nonlinearly, with performance roughly flat through a cool optimum near 10--15\,\textdegree C wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and degrading increasingly above it. The cost is not the same for everyone. Slower runners in the mass field lose two to three times more pace per degree than the elite field does. That gradient appears to reflect longer time on course, larger body size, and habitual climate rather than finishing position itself, and the penalty grows with race distance. Sensitivity also depends on what a runner has recently trained in. Over 5--14 days of exposure the body adapts, expanding plasma volume and lowering heart rate and core temperature at a given effort, and that adaptation decays within one to a few weeks once exposure stops. Large observational studies rarely model this individual variation directly, handling it through geographic proxies if at all, while the controlled experiments that do measure it individually run at samples of 10--30 participants and seldom connect to race performance.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Methods.}} We assembled an observational corpus of roughly a quarter million Garmin training runs, each enriched with the weather it was run in. A transformer trained on each runner's recent streams predicts pace and, through counterfactual WBGT sweeps, yields two heat features: a population heat curve and a per-runner sensitivity score. We add these to a weather-blind XGBoost pace model (heart rate, fitness, distance, and elevation) and evaluate on a runner-disjoint held-out set of 15{,}892 activities from 104 runners, using robust MAPE and signed bias with runner-clustered bootstrap intervals, plus a direct affine calibration of the heat features.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Results.}} Adding the population heat curve lowered robust held-out error modestly (MAPE from 7.34 to 7.29\%), and adding the per-runner sensitivity score lowered it further (to 7.13\%), with a shuffled control confirming that the personalized gain is runner-specific. The population curve is well calibrated, compatible with what the model needs. The personalized score provides a genuine predictive signal, mostly acting as a runner-level calibration offset rather than a temperature interaction. This effect is nearly flat across WBGT, only about 55\% survives when forced through the temperature curve, and its residual direction is reversed, plausibly because heart rate in the baseline already carries heat sensitivity.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{\textsc{Implications.}} A population heat adjustment can be applied to finish-time predictions now, letting runners see how forecast conditions are likely to change their expected pace; it is well calibrated and agrees with an independent coaching heuristic. Individualized heat correction is predictive but not yet a directly applicable adjustment, and remains a question for future work, pending a baseline with an explicit mediation structure and, above all, race-day performance labels. The appropriate accuracy metric is calibration on hot runs rather than average error, because the heat effect is small next to day-to-day training variation.

\end{singlespace}

\section{Introduction}

Anthropogenic climate change has at least doubled the likelihood of record-breaking hot years globally since the late 1960s \citep{king2017}. Over the same period the share of global land area affected by moderate-to-extreme heat waves tripled, and severe heat waves are projected to recur biannually in major population centers by the century's end \citep{russo2014}.

Marathons expose tens of thousands of runners to whatever conditions race day delivers, and the six World Marathon Majors (Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago, New York City, and Tokyo) show a marked increase in the frequency of unusually hot editions. As Figure~\ref{fig:marathon_starts_trend} shows, only three of the 211 races held between 1980 and 2019 exceeded a wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) of 65 °F (18 °C), a rate of about 0.08 per year. Three of the 29 races held between 2020 and 2026 already have, a rate of roughly 0.43 per year and a sixfold increase.\footnote{WBGT was estimated using Open-Meteo reanalysis with the Stull (2011) wet-bulb formula and a globe-temperature approximation following \citet{hunter1999}. Fixing the lookup at 10\,am local gives a consistent cross-decade comparison but does not perfectly match on-course exposure, since gun times have shifted over the decades.} The Chicago Marathon is the clearest individual case, with three of its hottest editions falling in the 2020s.

Those conditions confound an already difficult prediction problem. An accurate projection matters to the millions who attempt the distance each year, since it underwrites pacing, goal-setting, and training adjustment. Classic practitioner formulas, including Yasso 800s and VDOT \citep{yasso2008life, gaudette2012yasso, daniels2013running}, remain unpublished in peer-reviewed form decades after their inception. The growth of GPS watches among runners, which track their position, heart rate, and altitude consistently throughout training, offers exciting new pathways for generalizable computational models. 

What every one of these approaches shares, including the predictors now shipped with consumer running platforms \citep{garmin_race_predictor, strava_performance_predictions}, is that it is weather-blind. \citet{vernon2021} documented the cost directly: at the warm 2018 London Marathon (24\,\textdegree C maximum), mass-participation runners finished roughly 47 minutes slower than their pre-race expectations on average, about 14\% off target. A predictor that ignores race-day conditions is therefore systematically optimistic on precisely the days when a runner most needs a dependable estimate.

This paper asks whether that missing weather term can be learned from passively collected training data. We assemble an observational corpus of roughly a quarter of a million Garmin training runs, each matched to the weather in which it was run, and train a transformer on each runner's recent training streams to predict pace. Sweeping temperature counterfactually through the trained model yields two heat features: a population heat curve, describing the average pace penalty as conditions warm, and a per-runner sensitivity score. We then test whether either feature improves a weather-blind pace model on runners held out entirely from training, and we calibrate both against the correction that model actually requires. Section~\ref{sec:literature} synthesizes the evidence on heat effects and acclimation that motivates this design, Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data and the held-out experiment, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws out their implications and what remains open.

\section{How Heat Affects Endurance Running Performance}
\label{sec:literature}

Heat degrades endurance running performance through a chain that begins with a physical constraint on heat dissipation, passes through cardiovascular and central strain, and ends in a pacing decision. This section follows that chain, then turns to how the resulting effect has been measured, how it varies across race distances, and how it varies across runners.

Running is thermally expensive. Roughly 75 to 80\% of metabolic energy turnover during exercise appears as heat rather than as mechanical work. That heat must either be dissipated or stored in body tissue \citep{maughan2007}. Storage raises core temperature, and the margin available is narrow, since enzymatic function is near optimal around 39\,\textdegree C while deterioration sets in above roughly 40.5\,\textdegree C \citep{martin2007}. Competitive runners operate close to that ceiling in the heat. Among finishers of the 2019 World Athletics road races, held at WBGT between 23.5 and 30.6\,\textdegree C, 16\% completed their event with a core temperature above 40\,\textdegree C, with a peak recorded value of 40.7\,\textdegree C \citep{aylwin2023}.

The body sheds this heat mainly by evaporating sweat. Environmental conditions create the ceiling on how much evaporation is possible. Dry exchange through radiation and convection depends on the gradient between skin and air. That gradient reverses once air temperature exceeds skin temperature at roughly 36\,\textdegree C, beyond which the runner gains heat from the environment rather than losing it \citep{cheuvront2001}. Humidity constrains the evaporative route directly. Modeling world-record performances at 33\,\textdegree C and 50\% relative humidity, \citet{taylor2006} calculated an average required evaporation of about 4{,}200\,kJ against a maximum achievable evaporation of only 1{,}100\,kJ, so barely a quarter of the sweat needed for thermal balance could actually evaporate. Sweat produced beyond that ceiling drips rather than cools, and represents fluid lost for no thermal return. Radiation adds a load that air temperature does not capture at all. The difference of about 73\,W\,m$^{-2}$ that \citet{cheuvront2021} measured between early and late Boston Marathon start windows is equivalent to roughly one metabolic equivalent of extra heat production for a four-hour finisher.

The strain this imposes is cardiovascular before it is thermal, as skin blood flow directed to the surface for cooling competes with muscle for a finite cardiac output. Dehydration compounds the problem: beyond a 2\% loss of body weight, each further 1\% is associated with roughly a 0.4\,\textdegree C rise in rectal temperature during prolonged running. Sweat rates scale with conditions as well, averaging 0.83\,L\,h$^{-1}$ in marathons below 15\,\textdegree C against 1.2\,L\,h$^{-1}$ above it \citep{cheuvront2001}.

For a competitive runner, these strains express themselves in pace. In a simulated half marathon at WBGT 25.8\,\textdegree C, running speed and oxygen uptake fell significantly once core temperature reached about 39\,\textdegree C, which occurred between 6 and 9\,km, after which core temperature plateaued for the remainder of the race \citep{rodrigues2020}. Real race data corroborate this finding. \citet{ely2008} found that the gap between a runner's closing and opening 5\,km pace widened systematically as conditions warmed. \citet{maughan2007} report that post-marathon core temperature correlates with the time for the final 10\,km rather than with the overall finishing time. Heat therefore acts less as a fixed penalty spread evenly across a race than as a constraint that tightens as the race proceeds. Core temperature climbs toward a ceiling over the opening miles and is then held near it by the runner conceding speed, so the cost accumulates and is paid most heavily in the closing stages.

A purely mechanistic account nonetheless overstates the damage by a wide margin. Applying heat-balance reasoning to the conditions of the 1996 Atlanta Olympic marathon, at 35\,\textdegree C and 80\% relative humidity, Nielsen predicted that no runner could finish faster than about 3 hours and 20 minutes, whereas the men's race was won in 2:12:36 and the women's in 2:26:05 \citep{maughan2007, maughan2010}. Trained runners tolerate uncompensable conditions far better than a heat-balance calculation implies, partly by accepting a rising core temperature and partly through adaptations the model does not represent. That gap is the reason the applied literature estimates heat effects statistically from race outcomes rather than deriving them from thermal physics alone (which this paper does as well).

Estimating the effect empirically requires a scale, and the field has converged on wet-bulb globe temperature, which combines the three loads just described into one index and outperforms raw air temperature in cross-event comparison \citep{mantzios2022, ely2007, aylwin2023}. The standard formula is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WBGT} = 0.7\,T_{\mathrm{nwb}} + 0.2\,T_{\mathrm{bg}} + 0.1\,T_{\mathrm{db}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{nwb}}$ is the natural wet-bulb temperature, measured by a thermometer with a wet cloth wick ventilated by ambient wind and so capturing humidity and air movement together, $T_{\mathrm{bg}}$ is the black-globe temperature, measured inside a matte-black copper sphere and so capturing radiant load, and $T_{\mathrm{db}}$ is ambient air temperature \citep{cheuvront2021}. The weighting follows the physiology, giving most weight to the humidity-sensitive term because evaporative capacity is the binding constraint and dry heat alone is comparatively benign. The empirical gain is real but moderate. Across World Athletics events \citet{mantzios2022} found air temperature to be the single most important weather variable by feature importance, at 40\% against 26\% for relative humidity, 18\% for solar radiation and 16\% for wind, yet WBGT alone explained more variance than air temperature alone, with $R^2$ between 0.11 and 0.47 against 0.04 to 0.34.

The fitted shape of the heat penalty remains contested. Linear models dominate the regression literature and describe the moderate ranges observed at most major marathons adequately \citep{ely2007, knechtle2019, nikolaidis2019, vernon2021}. They cannot, however, represent the cool-end optimum that appears whenever a study spans genuinely cold conditions, and several designs recover exactly that. \citet{martin1999} fit men's New York winning times as $\mathrm{WT}(\mathrm{min}) = 148.51 - 0.713\,T + 0.00657\,T^2$ with $T$ in \textdegree F, minimised at 54.1\,\textdegree F or about 12\,\textdegree C, and noted that deterioration is especially marked beyond 75\,\textdegree F. \citet{elhelou2012} fit quadratics across six major marathons and recovered optima between 3.8 and 9.9\,\textdegree C depending on performance level, with a matching quadratic for withdrawal rate. \citet{gasparetto2020} fit cluster-specific quadratics in WBGT, with linear terms from $-58.75$ to $-202.9$\,s\,\textdegree C$^{-1}$ and quadratic terms from 3.79 to 13.03\,s\,\textdegree C$^{-2}$, locating a tipping point near 8\,\textdegree C WBGT. \citet{llewellyn2025} described a J-curve for the median runner with an inflection near 10\,\textdegree C. The largest synthesis is explicitly piecewise rather than quadratic: across 1{,}258 elite races \citet{mantzios2022} reported a flat optimum band of 7.5 to 15\,\textdegree C WBGT with performance declining by 0.4 $\pm$ 0.4\% per \textdegree C above it and 0.3 $\pm$ 0.2\% per \textdegree C below it.

Two studies supply forms usable directly as priors. \citet{aylwin2023} fit percent of personal best against WBGT across elite World Championship road races as $\%\mathrm{PB} = 2.1534\,\mathrm{WBGT} + 52.513$, with $R^2 = 0.89$. \citet{vernon2021} fit London mean finishing time twice, once on race-day maximum temperature as $1.5248\,T_{\max} + 246.52$ and once on the differential between race-day maximum and the prior 60-day mean as $1.4918\,\Delta T + 265.54$, the second of which is the closest thing in the corpus to an acclimation-aware predictor. Alongside these sit older practitioner rules of similar magnitude, including Frederick's roughly 2\% per 5\,\textdegree C of deviation from 12\,\textdegree C, Daniels' 1.6\% per 5\,\textdegree C up to 32\,\textdegree C, and Brown's 7 to 8\% in warm conditions \citep{montain2007}.

The estimated optimum is where these forms disagree most visibly, spanning 3.8 to 9.9\,\textdegree C air temperature \citep{elhelou2012}, 5 to 10\,\textdegree C \citep{llewellyn2025}, 10 to 12\,\textdegree C \citep{maughan2010}, about 12\,\textdegree C \citep{martin1999}, 12 to 15\,\textdegree C \citep{martin2007} and 7.5 to 15\,\textdegree C WBGT \citep{mantzios2022}. Part of that spread is the scale, since WBGT and air temperature are not interchangeable, and part is the population, since the optimum shifts with performance level. The most direct challenge comes from \citet{scheer2021}, who found that men's marathon world records were set at a mean ambient temperature of 18.61 $\pm$ 2.59\,\textdegree C, well above every published optimum, with the record at the time set at 21.4\,\textdegree C. Record conditions reflect the joint distribution of fast fields, fast courses and race scheduling rather than the thermal optimum alone, but the finding is a useful caution against reading a sharply peaked optimum from aggregate data. Section~\ref{sec:results} places the curve estimated here against this set.

Heat effects also depend strongly on the event, and the clearest evidence spans a wider range of distances than the marathon literature alone. Comparing the top performances at seven consecutive World Championships from 1999 to 2011, classified as temperate below 25\,\textdegree C or hot at or above it, \citet{guy2014} found that the sign of the effect reverses across the programme. Sprinters were substantially faster in the heat, with large effect sizes of $d = 2.4$ and $2.3$ for men at 100 and 200\,m, while endurance events were slower, and the marathon was the most impaired of all, at $-3.1\%$ for men ($d = -2.0$) and $-2.7\%$ for women ($d = -2.4$). Heat is therefore not uniformly harmful to running; it is harmful to running that lasts long enough for heat storage to matter.

The heat-balance modelling in \citet{taylor2006} supplies the reason. Because stored heat is the accumulated difference between production and dissipation, its effect is an integral over the duration of the effort, and a short event simply does not last long enough for core temperature to approach the limiting range. Modelling world-record performances from 100\,m to the marathon, that analysis found events up to 1500\,m to be unaffected by thermal strain, a range that extends to 5000\,m and possibly 10{,}000\,m at 23\,\textdegree C, with meaningful body-temperature elevation confined to the longer events. Sprints gain from warmth for unrelated reasons, including muscle temperature and reduced air density, and lose nothing thermally because there is no time to store appreciable heat.

Within the endurance events the ordering is less clean than a simple duration argument predicts. The event-specific slopes in \citet{mantzios2022} are approximately 0.2\% per \textdegree C WBGT for the marathon, 0.3\% for 5000\,m and 0.04\% for 10{,}000\,m, an ordering that is not monotone in distance. The racewalking events, which last longer than any running event on the programme, show the steepest slopes of all at roughly 0.4\% for 20\,km and 1.1\% for 50\,km. The racewalking gradient is consistent with duration being the operative variable, whereas the track results are noisy, being based on far fewer championship races and on events where tactical racing rather than time is often the objective. Direct same-day comparisons across distances under matched fitness are essentially absent from the literature. The prudent reading is that heat penalty increases with the duration of the effort, strongly and reliably between sprint and marathon, with the finer ordering among endurance events not well resolved.

The final source of variation is the runner. The most studied axis is performance level, and across nearly every large observational study slower runners lose more pace per degree than faster ones. \citet{ely2007} found that between the coolest and warmest WBGT quartiles the 25th, 50th, 100th and 300th finishers slowed by 2.6, 3.5, 4.9 and 7.9\% respectively, which is roughly 1.1 to 3.2\% per 5\,\textdegree C WBGT across that range. At Boston, \citet{nikolaidis2019} estimated about 1 minute 53 seconds of slowing per \textdegree C for all finishers against 38 seconds for the top ten, and \citet{knechtle2019} reported 1 minute 47 seconds per \textdegree C for all finishers against 20 seconds for annual winners, with the top-ten effect not statistically distinguishable from zero. \citet{knechtle2021b} found the hottest New York temperature tertile added 7.73 minutes for men overall but only 1.87 minutes for top-ten finishers, and \citet{vihma2010} calculated that warming from 10 to 25\,\textdegree C costs an elite male about 5 minutes against 23 minutes for a four-hour finisher. In tropical Singapore, \citet{tan2022} recovered the same ordering, with a dry-bulb coefficient of 10.48 for the lower-performance cluster against 6.87 for the higher.

That regularity has real exceptions, and they are informative. \citet{gasparetto2020} found the opposite ordering in their cluster analysis, where moving from 8 to 20\,\textdegree C WBGT cost the fastest cluster 16.89\% and the second cluster 21.89\% but the slowest cluster, comprising about three quarters of the sample, only 5.44\%. \citet{knechtle2021a} found the correlation between temperature and finishing time reverses sign for the top three finishers, who ran faster as temperature rose. \citet{ely2008} found that closing pace deteriorated with warmth for winners and 25th-place finishers but showed no temperature dependence at 100th place. These patterns are consistent with slower runners in some fields pacing conservatively from the outset and thereby absorbing heat as a smaller marginal loss, and they caution that the gradient is a property of how a field is stratified rather than a fixed physiological law. Even the shape is disputed, with \citet{ely2007} describing an accelerating penalty across finishing position and \citet{vihma2010} finding the relative increment approximately linear in finishing time.

Several mechanisms have been proposed for the gradient, and they are not the same variable. Slower runners are on course longer and accumulate more of the day's thermal and solar load, often through the hottest part of the morning. Body morphology contributes independently: in a study of marathoners with laboratory-measured fitness, the ratio of body surface area to mass predicted relative performance decrement in warm conditions, reaching $r = 0.974$ within the fittest subgroup in the warmest year studied, and percent body fat predicted decrement at $r = 0.503$ \citep{roach2012}. Metabolic heat production works in the opposite direction to the observed gradient, which is worth stating plainly, since faster runners generate more heat and finish hotter. Across marathon studies, post-race rectal temperature correlates with $\dot{V}\mathrm{O}_2$max at $r = 0.777$ and with running speed at $r = 0.585$, while dehydration does not predict it at all \citep{cheuvront2001}. Elite runners therefore tolerate a larger thermal load while losing less time, which points to tolerance rather than to a lighter heat burden. Field density matters too, since running in a pack can amplify physiological heat stress roughly threefold relative to running alone \citep{montain2007}.

Habitual climate is a further axis, and its effect is not monotone. At the Marathon des Sables, athletes from warmer countries ran 10.7\% faster overall than those from colder countries, yet athletes from the hottest countries of origin, at 25 to 35\,\textdegree C, gained no advantage over those from moderately warm countries at 15 to 25\,\textdegree C and performed similarly to the coldest group \citep{ioannou2018}. Whether that inverted pattern reflects a ceiling on useful acclimatisation or the confounding of origin climate with training resources cannot be settled from the design, but it argues against treating habitual heat exposure as a simple linear advantage.

Sex and age have been examined repeatedly with inconsistent results. \citet{elhelou2012} found women on average 10.3 $\pm$ 1.6\% slower than men with the gap stable across temperatures, implying no sex difference in heat sensitivity, and \citet{knechtle2021b} found no significant temperature-by-sex interaction ($p = 0.17$) even while wind and humidity interactions were significant. Against this, \citet{roach2012} found women ran further from their predicted time than men in warm conditions, \citet{llewellyn2025} found a significant temperature association for top-ten women in only one of six marathons against four of six for men, and \citet{knechtle2021a} found the sex effect reversing between the top ten and the top hundred. For age, both \citet{knechtle2021b} and \citet{weiss2022a} report the largest heat effects in middle-aged runners, roughly 30 to 64 for men and 40 to 64 for women, with no comparable effect in runners over 70, a pattern that plausibly reflects the pace and racing intensity of those groups rather than age itself.

Taken together, these axes are best understood as observable proxies for an underlying and largely unmeasured individual heat tolerance. Finishing position, body composition, country of origin, sex and age each capture part of it and none measures it directly, and the observational designs that recover them cannot separate tolerance from pacing strategy, fitness or field composition. This motivates the approach taken here, which estimates a runner-specific heat response from that runner's own training history rather than from demographic proxies. It also motivates the next section, since the one component of heat tolerance that is known to be dynamic, acquirable and reversible is acclimation.

Other weather variables have received far less attention and show effects that are secondary to heat, frequently confounded with temperature or course geometry, and sometimes signed counterintuitively because of that confounding \citep{nikolaidis2019, knechtle2019, vihma2010, weiss2024}. They are noted here for completeness and are not pursued further.

\subsection{Heat Acclimation}

Repeated exposure to heat produces a coordinated set of adaptations that make the same effort thermally cheaper. Plasma volume expands by roughly 7--18\%, resting core temperature falls, sweating begins earlier and loses less sodium, and skin blood flow responds sooner, so at a fixed workload an acclimatised runner shows a lower heart rate, a lower core temperature, and a lower perceived effort together \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, racinais2017, gibson2020}. The practical consequence is thermoregulatory headroom, which is to say the ability to hold a given pace longer before it has to drop \citep{marino2008, rodrigues2020}. The adaptation can be induced three ways: by exercising in the heat, by passive heating such as sauna or hot-water immersion, or simply by living and training in a hot climate. All three work over a span of about 5--14 days, with hot-climate residence combined with training producing the most complete response \citep{garrett2011, periard2015, tyler2016, tyler2024, heathcote2018, pryor2019, schmit2017, brown2022}.

What matters for modeling is not only that the adaptation exists but how quickly it arrives and how quickly it fades, and both are now reasonably well characterized. Heart-rate and core-temperature adaptations develop fastest, reaching about 75\% of their eventual gain within 4--7 days; plasma volume expands over 3--6 days; sweat-rate adaptations continue to develop through 10--14 days \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, racinais2020, chalmers2014, cubel2024}. Decay runs on a similar order once exposure stops, at roughly 2.5\% per day for the heart-rate channel, though re-acclimation is considerably faster than initial induction \citep{daanen2018, racinais2020, pryor2019}. A block of 10--14 days therefore captures most of the available adaptation, and a single session every 5--7 days maintains it \citep{daanen2018, garrett2011}. A runner's acclimation state can accordingly be approximated from training-temperature history over the trailing two to four weeks, weighting recent sessions more heavily. Figure~\ref{fig:acclimation} plots these time courses alongside the two-timescale weighting used here to summarize recent exposure, which follows the relative ordering of the reported decay rates rather than their absolute coefficients, since the onset and decay regressions of \citet{daanen2018} reference physiological channels that training-stream data does not measure.

Large-sample observational studies rarely model any of this explicitly. Most handle heat adaptation through geographic or fitness proxies such as climate of origin or tropical residence, and almost none fit an individual-level acclimation state \citep{ioannou2018, scheer2021, knechtle2021a, tan2022}. The exception is \citet{vernon2021}, who computed the difference between race-day maximum temperature and each runner's rolling 60-day prior training-environment temperature and reported a correlation of $r = 0.85$ between that differential and mean finish time, with about 2.6\% additional slowdown per 5 °C of positive differential. Two cautions attach to that figure. It is computed at the race-mean level, describing how average finish time tracks the average differential, and ecological correlations of this kind typically run far larger than the individual-runner association they are read as implying. It also comes from a single race, the warm 2018 London. The differential is nonetheless the closest the corpus comes to an individual-level acclimation feature, and it is directly aligned with the approach taken here.

Controlled experiments work at the individual level by construction, measuring adaptation before and after an intervention in each participant, but they carry samples of roughly 10--30 and seldom connect those measurements to race-day performance. The two literatures thus leave a gap between physiological precision at small $n$ and statistical power without any individual-level adaptation term. The model developed here sits in that gap. It learns a runner-specific heat response from a training history that already spans the range of conditions each runner has encountered, at a scale the controlled studies cannot reach, with the onset and decay rates above informing how that history is summarized.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The results in this paper come from a single held-out experiment: whether a deep-learning model's learned heat response improves a weather-blind pace model, first as a population temperature feature and then as runner-specific heat information. This section describes the training corpus, the deep-learning model that produces the heat features, the weather-blind baseline that consumes them, and the held-out evaluation. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} is the map.

\subsection{The training corpus}

The corpus is observational: real training runs recorded on Garmin devices, not a controlled experiment. Every outdoor run is enriched with the weather it was actually run in, queried from Open-Meteo at the run's time and location, so the table spans the United States, Great Britain, India, France, Australia, and more, and the hot end of the WBGT range is genuinely populated. The full model frame is about 259{,}000 activities from 1{,}628 runners. The held-out experiment reported here uses a runner-disjoint slice of 15{,}892 activities from 104 runners, none of whom were used to fit any model. Two facts shape the analysis. There are no race labels: these are training runs at training effort, so the results describe held-out prediction, not a causal physiological estimate. And this particular held-out cohort spans WBGT from about $-18$ to $25\,^{\circ}$C, so the hottest bins carry more sampling uncertainty than the mild ones.

We summarize each run's WBGT by its time-weighted mean over the run rather than its starting value, because a runner who starts at 6\,am in the cool and finishes in the heat should be scored by the middle of that curve. The deep model uses a radiation-aware outdoor WBGT, computed from the measured shortwave radiation and wind and solved for globe temperature with \texttt{thermofeel}; a closed-form shade approximation is used only where a value must be reconstructed from the familiar quantities a user would supply, namely air temperature and humidity.

\subsection{The deep-learning heat model}

The heat features come from a transformer trained on each runner's recent training streams, a factored-attention network in the style of an existing fitness-prediction model (checkpoint \texttt{singleC\_q100\_s1\_500k}). It reads each activity as a stack of per-second channels (pace, heart rate, distance, cadence, altitude, and WBGT), tokenizes and embeds each channel, sums them with a $1/\sqrt{C}$ scale, and passes them through attention that mixes information within a run and across the runner's recent runs. It predicts pace directly (Figure~\ref{fig:dlarch}). Two heat artifacts are read off the trained model by counterfactual WBGT sweeps that hold everything else fixed.

The \emph{population heat curve} is the median predicted pace penalty as WBGT is swept, anchored at $10\,^{\circ}$C and estimated from the DL-training runners only (1{,}186 runners, with a percentile reference of 750 runners, one averaged value each so prolific runners do not dominate). Its predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C is about 5.0\%.

The \emph{per-runner sensitivity score} is each runner's percentile rank of DL-predicted slowdown at $28\,^{\circ}$C relative to $10\,^{\circ}$C, $\mathrm{slowdown}_{28} = 100\,(\mathrm{DL}_{28^\circ}/\mathrm{DL}_{10^\circ} - 1)$. The primary, prospective version uses a strict prior of exactly 15 preceding windows and excludes the target activity, so it is fully prospective.

\subsection{The weather-blind baseline and the feature progression}

The baseline is a weather-blind XGBoost pace model with no temperature input: current-run average heart rate, trailing 30-day fitted pace, current-run distance, and elevation (gain and grade). The experiment adds the deep-learning heat information in two steps. The population step adds the DL population curve's value at the run's WBGT as one feature, which the tree model is free to use or ignore. The personalized step additionally adds the runner's sensitivity score. Two controls sharpen the reading. A \emph{shuffled control} reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, so any genuine personalization gain should vanish. A \emph{constrained variant} supplies only the percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, which forces personalization to act through the temperature curve rather than as a free runner-level number. A supplementary branch (Section~\ref{sec:results}) replaces the heart-rate-and-trailing-pace baseline with the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, to test whether the findings survive a deliberately different, pre-run baseline.

\subsection{Evaluation and calibration tests}

Every comparison uses the same 15{,}892-activity holdout, the same XGBoost hyperparameters, and three seeds (100, 200, 300). Error is reported as robust MAPE (clipped at its 99th percentile) and signed bias (clipped at its 1st and 99th percentiles), applied identically to every model, with runner-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals. Beyond prediction error, we run a direct calibration test of whether the DL curves point the right way. We fit an affine correction to the weather-blind prediction, $\mathrm{pace} = \hat{p}_0\,(1 + \beta + \alpha\,c_{\mathrm{pop}} + \gamma\,d_{\mathrm{pers}})$, where $c_{\mathrm{pop}}$ is the population curve and $d_{\mathrm{pers}}$ the personalized deviation, and read two coefficients: $\alpha$, the scale of the population curve, where a value near 1 means the curve is already the right size, and $\gamma$, the direction of the personalized deviation, where a positive value means the saved DL sensitivity points the right way.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the held-out experiment in order: the main model comparison, how the error varies with temperature, how the learned heat curve compares to external references, what the personalization actually is, a sensitivity check under a different baseline, and the complementary acclimation evidence. Table~\ref{tab:mainresult} collects the headline numbers.

Adding the deep-learning heat information to the weather-blind XGBoost improves held-out accuracy, and a negative control confirms that the personalized part is real (Figure~\ref{fig:mape_ladder}). Robust MAPE falls from 7.3381\% for the weather-blind model, to 7.2936\% when the population DL curve is added (a gain of 0.0445 points), to 7.1304\% when the per-runner sensitivity score is added on top (a further 0.1632 points, and 0.2077 points below the weather-blind model). The current-inclusive, prior, and strict-prior versions of the personalized score perform almost identically, so the gain is not an artifact of including the target activity, and the strict-prior version is the appropriate prospective estimate.

The controls behave as they should. The shuffled control, which reassigns each runner's sensitivity score to a random runner, lands at 7.2996\%, essentially tied with the population model, so the personalization gain depends on matching the score to the correct runner. In runner-clustered bootstrap terms, the personalized gain beyond the population curve is 0.1632 points with a 95\% interval of [0.039, 0.308], which excludes zero, while the shuffled control's interval [$-0.051$, 0.034] straddles it. The population curve's own gain over the weather-blind model, 0.0445 points, has an interval of [$-0.009$, 0.101] that narrowly includes zero, so the population feature is a modest and only marginally significant improvement on this cohort.

Stratifying the held-out error by WBGT shows where each model helps (Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp}). The weather-blind model has the classic pattern: it predicts too slow in cold and mild conditions (a signed bias near $-2\%$) and swings to too fast in the hottest bin (a bias of $+0.75\%$ in the 22 to 25\,\textdegree C band, meaning it over-predicts pace on hot runs). The population DL feature lowers MAPE across the range, including the hottest bin. The personalized model lowers MAPE in every temperature bin and places the signed bias closest to zero throughout. Its bias curve is nearly parallel to the population model's, however, which is the first sign that the personalized gain is a level shift rather than a temperature interaction. The hottest bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones, so those estimates carry more sampling uncertainty.

The deep model's population heat curve can be compared to the published literature without any shared fitting (Figure~\ref{fig:heateffects}). Expressed as percent slowdown versus a $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, the published forms fall into two bands: steep slopes for amateur and mass fields (\citealp{ely2007, nikolaidis2019, knechtle2019, vernon2021}, reaching 10 to 16\% by $30\,^{\circ}$C) and shallow slopes for elite fields (\citealp{mantzios2022, martin1999, guy2014}, near 3 to 5\%). Our curve sits at the lower edge of this range, close to the elite and shallow-amateur forms and reaching about 5\% by $28\,^{\circ}$C, which is the expected place for a mixed-effort cohort whose runs are mostly training rather than racing. It also agrees with an independent coaching heuristic, which reaches about 5.6\% at $28\,^{\circ}$C, near the deep model's 72nd percentile.

The personalized score clearly improves prediction, but a set of diagnostics shows that its improvement is mostly a runner-level calibration offset rather than a heat-specific interaction, and that its residual direction is in fact reversed. Three pieces of evidence point the same way.

First, the change the personalized score makes to the prediction is nearly flat across WBGT. Relative to the population model, it shifts the prediction by about $-0.4$ to $-0.6\%$ across temperature bins, with essentially no row-level association with WBGT ($r = -0.027$); the change is instead ordered by the runner's DL-score quintile. That is why the temperature-bin curves in Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_temp} are parallel.

Second, forcing the personalization to act through the temperature curve keeps only part of the gain. When personalization is supplied as a percentile-matched deviation of the DL curve, so that its input is zero at and below the $10\,^{\circ}$C anchor, the model reaches 7.2043\% robust MAPE, a gain of 0.0893 points beyond the population curve (95\% interval [0.015, 0.167], which excludes zero) with the shuffled version showing no lift. That is a real, temperature-shaped signal, but it is about 55\% of the 0.1632-point gain of the free runner rank. The remaining advantage of the free rank is consistent with general runner-level calibration information that is not specifically temperature-dependent.

Third, a direct calibration of the DL curves against what the weather-blind model actually needs settles the direction (Figure~\ref{fig:gamma}). The population curve's scale coefficient is $\alpha = 1.078$, with a 95\% interval of [0.823, 1.344] that includes one, so the population curve is already close to the right size. The personalized deviation's coefficient is $\gamma = -1.654$, with a 95\% interval of [$-2.545$, $-0.865$] that is decisively negative. It is nearly identical across the three seeds and stays negative when the calibration is restricted to progressively hotter subsets. Conditional on the population curve, then, runners assigned a larger saved DL slowdown require a correction in the \emph{opposite} direction. The saved personalized curves are therefore not calibrated as a direct, expected-direction multiplicative layer on top of this model, which also explains why a flexible tree model can still use them, since it is free to learn the inverse mapping.

This reversal is a statement about the residual after conditioning on the baseline, not proof that the deep model's physiological ordering is backwards. The weather-blind baseline already includes average heart rate and 30-day fitted pace, and both may already encode the consequences of heat sensitivity. A genuinely heat-sensitive runner may already receive a slower prediction through an elevated heart rate, which leaves an oppositely signed residual for the personalized feature to absorb. The defensible statement is that the population DL curve is well scaled, while the personalized DL deviation is reproducible predictive information whose residual-layer association is reversed, so it should not be applied directly as an expected-direction heat multiplier without reconsidering the baseline features and the mediation structure.

Because the reversal described above is conditional on a heart-rate-aware baseline, we re-ran the whole progression on a deliberately different baseline that removes current effort: the fixed, independently-trained marathon-fitness estimate plus distance and elevation, with current-run heart rate and trailing pace excluded. This branch is supplementary and cannot be read as an apples-to-apples fitness comparison, since it changes the fitness representation and drops current effort at the same time, which raises its absolute MAPE. Within the branch, the population temperature information remains useful (though its gain interval includes zero), raw WBGT produces a clearer gain, and the strict-prior personalization no longer improves beyond the population curve. The personalized $\gamma$ reversal disappears under this baseline, but the estimate is imprecise and unstable across runner folds. The reading is that the personalized result depends on the conditional baseline, and that this branch does not independently validate the personalized magnitude.

As complementary evidence, the deep model's per-runner sensitivity tracks recent heat exposure in the acclimation-consistent direction: runners with more recent hot training receive a lower sensitivity rank, and an independently curated set of matched hot runs improves after a block of heat exposure on average. These are candidate acclimation episodes, not verified physiological labels, and the held-out cohort is not large or repeatable enough near $28\,^{\circ}$C to validate individual adaptation magnitudes. The signal is consistent with acclimation being a real population-level effect while remaining too noisy to resolve as a per-person tracker.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section draws out the implications of the results for heat-adjusted pace prediction, then their limitations.

\subsection{Implications}

A weather-adjusted pace prediction lets a runner see how a given day's heat is likely to change the finish time they would otherwise expect from their fitness, and lets them explore how a warmer or cooler forecast would move that expectation. The results show that the population component of this correction is ready for such use. The deep model's population heat curve is well scaled, since its calibration coefficient is compatible with one, it modestly improves held-out prediction, and it agrees with an independent coaching heuristic and with the published literature. Individualized heat correction is a harder problem. The per-runner sensitivity score carries reproducible predictive information, but on a heart-rate-aware baseline its residual direction is reversed, so it cannot yet be applied as a direct, expected-direction adjustment. The honest reading is that a population heat adjustment can be applied to finish-time predictions now, while a validated per-runner heat adjustment awaits further work.

Several additions would sharpen the picture. A labeled race set is the most valuable, because the corpus is training runs and the experiment can only measure held-out prediction, not race-day calibration directly. A baseline whose mediation structure is explicit enough to interpret the personalized direction would resolve whether the reversed coefficient reflects heart rate already carrying heat sensitivity or a genuine problem with the score. Denser sampling of genuinely hot runs would tighten the hot-end estimates, which are thin by design because hot races are cancelled or postponed. A few variables the literature flags are absent from raw GPX files but would plausibly improve prediction: body mass, since heat production scales with body mass and smaller runners are advantaged in heat \citep{marino2008, maughan2010}; biological sex, an established performance differential under heat \citep{llewellyn2025, vihma2010, kelly2023, weiss2022b}; and resting heart rate, one of the most validated acclimation markers \citep{periard2015, tyler2016, pryor2019}. Lab-only variables such as plasma volume or sweat sodium concentration cannot be obtained from consumer devices and are outside the scope of this approach. Throughout, the appropriate accuracy metric is calibration and signed bias on hot runs rather than average error over the full distribution, because most days are temperate and the heat effect is small next to run-to-run variation.

\subsection{Limitations}

The headline caveat is that this is runner-disjoint held-out prediction, not a causal estimate of heat's physiological effect. The corpus is training runs, so every number describes how well one model predicts held-out pace, and the improvements are calibration and accuracy gains rather than measured heat responses. The held-out cohort also spans WBGT only to about $25\,^{\circ}$C, and the hottest bins hold fewer activities than the mild ones, so the hot-end estimates carry the most sampling uncertainty. The reported robust MAPE and bias are clipped at the 99th (and 1st) percentiles precisely because a few large-error rows would otherwise dominate.

The personalized result is real but narrow. A runner-matched deep-learning score improves held-out prediction, and a shuffled control removes the gain, but in this specification the incremental effect is mostly temperature-independent, only about 55\% of it survives when forced through the temperature curve, and its residual-layer coefficient is reversed. Whether that reversal reflects mediation through the heart-rate and pace features in the baseline, rather than a backwards physiological ordering, cannot be settled here, because the sensitivity branch that removes current effort loses the reversal but is too imprecise to be decisive. The personalized DL score should therefore be treated as predictive runner-level information, not as a validated, directly applicable heat multiplier.

The acclimation evidence is complementary and suggestive rather than confirmatory. Recent exposure predicts lower DL sensitivity and curated hot runs improve after exposure, but the data cannot validate individual adaptation magnitudes near race-relevant heat. This document will be revised as a labeled race set becomes available and as the mediation structure of the baseline is made explicit enough to interpret the personalized calibration direction.
